# Agent Instructions

## Project purpose

Personal-IET is the working evidence repository for Philip McGaw's application for **IET Chartered Engineer (CEng) registration**. Agents must treat the repository as an evidence-gathering and application-preparation project, not simply as a CV or software workspace.

Work should help gather, organise, verify, and develop evidence for the CEng application. Distinguish source evidence from recollection, CV claims, proposals, and unverified statements. Preserve traceability, identify Philip's personal contribution, record outcomes and limitations, and explicitly record evidence gaps rather than inventing information.

The expected reader age for `Scouts Electronics Activity Badge Booklet.docx` is 10 to 16. Use this age range when reviewing or revising the booklet.

## Evidence integrity

These rules apply to all agent work on the CEng evidence and application, particularly when answering assessor questions or drafting first-person application text.

- **Never fill an evidence gap with a plausible assumption.** If a material fact is unknown, record it as unknown or ask Philip rather than completing the story.
- **Preserve provenance.** Distinguish clearly between retained documentary evidence, applicant-authored records, Philip's recollection, third-party statements, inference, proposed wording, and unresolved claims.
- **Do not convert an assessor question into a fact.** A question does not establish that the premise of the question is true.
- **Do not invent or silently infer dates, measurements, standards clauses, project outcomes, responsibilities, job titles, authority, customers, technical configurations, test results or commercial effects.**
- **General engineering knowledge is explanatory only.** It may be used to explain a technical mechanism, but must never be presented as evidence that Philip performed an action, made a decision, held responsibility, obtained a result or gained experience.
- **First-person claims require personal attribution.** Do not attribute team, supplier, customer or employer activity to Philip unless the available evidence or Philip's explicit recollection establishes his personal contribution.
- **Use Philip's voice in text presented as his own.** For public-facing writing, CV material, application answers and first-person case studies, use direct, natural language consistent with his wording and edits. Avoid generic editorial disclaimers or stock caveats; when a limitation matters, express it plainly in his voice (for example, “I haven't found a measurement record”). Keep evidence indexes, source notes and TODOs neutral where that best preserves provenance. Voice must not soften uncertainty or add unsupported claims.
- **Numerical claims require a basis.** A number must be traceable to a record or explicitly identified as Philip's recollection; do not manufacture precision from approximate information.
- **Conflicting evidence must be surfaced.** Do not choose the version that makes the application stronger. Record the conflict and resolve it from the strongest available source.
- **Draft wording is not evidence.** Generated prose, summaries and proposed case studies must not subsequently be treated as source evidence merely because they are written confidently.
- **When evidence is insufficient, preserve the limitation.** A smaller, defensible claim is preferable to a broader unsupported one.
- **Ask before resolving material ambiguity.** If a missing fact could change the competence mapping, personal-contribution claim, outcome, or application wording, ask Philip rather than infer it.
- **Use explicit status labels where useful:** documented, applicant-reported/recollection, inferred, proposed, unresolved, or verified.
- **Do not mark evidence or TODO work complete until the supporting basis has actually been checked.**
- **Application integrity takes precedence over completeness.** The objective is an accurate, defensible CEng application, not filling every competence gap.

## Language and house style

- All newly authored content, documentation, comments, commit messages, and other human-readable project text must use British English unless a technical identifier, quotation, historical source, proper name, or external project's established spelling requires otherwise.
- Use British spelling and vocabulary, including `organisation`, `organise`, `licence` as a noun, `license` as a verb, `centre`, `colour`, and `behaviour`. Use `program` for computer software and `programme` for organised activities or broadcasts. Use Oxford commas consistently where they improve grammatical clarity.
- Write clearly, concisely, precisely, and directly. Prefer active voice and concrete terminology; follow the principles of *The Elements of Style* by removing unnecessary words and avoiding ambiguity and promotional language. In articles, retain conversational phrasing and personal asides where they convey experience, judgement, or humour.
- Use consistent capitalisation for project names, technologies, standards, organisations, and document terms. Do not introduce arbitrary title case.
- **Job-title spelling:** Use *principal* for a senior or leading role in titles such as *Principal Electrical Engineer*; *principle* means a fundamental rule or idea. Preserve the spelling used in documentary sources and flag apparent errors or discrepancies rather than silently correcting a recorded title.
- **Currency and monetary values:** Express all currency amounts in pounds sterling (GBP, £) by default, including prices, salaries, budgets, costs, and financial comparisons. Convert amounts quoted in other currencies to GBP; where the conversion matters, identify the exchange-rate date or state that the rate is current and approximate. Retain the original-currency amount alongside the GBP equivalent only when necessary for context or traceability.
- When adding images or illustrations, source them from non-AI sources and use a licence that permits the intended use. Check any attribution, modification, and redistribution conditions. Do not generate an AI image when a suitable Creative Commons-licensed image is available for the task.
- Preserve established technical terminology where it has a defined meaning. Do not silently Britishise quotations, source text, names, URLs, code, or identifiers. Keep editorial text distinct from quoted or historically preserved material; correct incidental errors in existing editorial text where this improves consistency, but do not rewrite historical source material merely to impose modern house style.
- Use Unicode characters where semantically correct and readable; do not substitute ASCII approximations unnecessarily.
- Write full dates in ISO format (`YYYY-MM-DD`). In prose, write month-and-year references as `April 2025`, rather than `2025-04`; for example, “retained April 2025 presentation”. Preserve numeric date formats where required by filenames, code, data, or external conventions.
- Follow NIST-style SI conventions: use the correct symbol and a non-breaking space between a numerical value and its unit or symbol, for example `10 mm`, `5 V`, and `25 °C`. Use `°C` for Celsius temperatures and correct Unicode symbols where applicable.
- Retain imperial units when reporting measurements from external sources, and provide an approximate SI equivalent in parentheses, for example, `12 in (approximately 305 mm)`. Round conversions to a precision appropriate to the source and context; do not imply greater accuracy than the original measurement supports. Preserve quotations verbatim and place conversions outside the quoted text.
- Use commas as thousands separators in ordinary English prose where needed for readability, for example `1,000`, while preserving technical and code formats that require another convention.
- Use `§` when referring to a section of the same document where appropriate.
- Use RFC 2119-style status words deliberately: `MUST`, `MUST NOT`, `SHOULD`, `SHOULD NOT`, and `MAY` indicate requirements or recommendations and must not be used casually.
- Use precise project-status terms such as `designed`, `planned`, `implemented`, `simulated`, `bench-tested`, `validated`, and `production-proven` according to the evidence. Do not describe work as validated or production-proven merely because it has been designed or implemented.
- Do not use American English merely because a tool, framework, template, or generated output does so. Retain a third-party product's official spelling while keeping surrounding prose in British English.

## Article writing voice

Apply this section when drafting or editing articles and other explanatory prose in Philip’s voice. The language and house style rules above still apply. For formal reports, specifications, and other structured documents, adapt the tone to their purpose while preserving clarity, precision, and honesty about the evidence.

- Write as a practising engineer sharing useful findings with a technically curious, capable reader.
- For technical explanations, use the requirements and principles described in Philip’s [article on Simplified Technical English](https://philipmcgaw.com/asd-ste100-clear-technical-english/) as a writing preference: use familiar, specific wording; keep terms consistent; and make sentence structure clear and direct. Follow Philip’s preference for British English. Preserve exact spelling in product names, quotations, source code, commands, and interface identifiers. Do not force controlled vocabulary or procedural rules into personal narrative, established technical terms, or asides that carry Philip’s voice. Where a document invokes BCP 14, preserve its uppercase key words and intended requirement force; do not replace them with ASD-STE100 dictionary alternatives if that changes the meaning. Clear wording does not establish technical or safety correctness: check instructions against the equipment, task, and applicable requirement. This preference is not a claim of full ASD-STE100 compliance; if full compliance is requested, check the current official specification, including its applicable writing rules and dictionary.
- Begin with the actual problem, motivation, or observation. Prefer specific context over a generic introduction to the topic.
- Use first person for Philip’s experiences, decisions, and preferences. Never invent experiences, opinions, tests, or results. If necessary facts are missing, ask for them or identify the gap in the draft.
- Explain what was tried, why it was chosen, and what happened. Include relevant constraints, trade-offs, unsuccessful approaches, and practical consequences.
- Ground explanations in useful details, such as components, settings, connections, measurements, and failure modes. Explain unfamiliar terms without labouring basics the intended reader can reasonably be expected to understand.
- Keep the tone conversational, matter-of-fact, and technically precise. Allow occasional dry humour, understated frustration, and short parenthetical asides where they arise naturally.
- Express supported opinions directly. Be firmer when explaining a consequential misconception, and show the reasoning behind the correction.
- State uncertainty plainly. Distinguish personal preference, inference, planned work, and observed results using the project-status terms defined above.
- Use headings, lists, tables, code, and diagrams where they help the reader understand or reproduce the work.
- End with the result, remaining limitation, or next useful step. Do not force a formal conclusion or repeat the article as a summary.
- When editing, preserve personal observations, judgement, and useful asides while correcting errors and tightening the prose. Do not reproduce typos or awkward grammar as features of the voice.
- Avoid marketing language, inflated claims, manufactured enthusiasm, forced jokes, and stock phrases.

The intended voice is practical, candid, mildly opinionated, and occasionally wry: an engineer explaining something from his own bench.

## Reusable professional-registration self-assessment

This repository and its evidence belong to Philip McGaw. The current checkout contains personal and potentially confidential material; it is not a generic sample project and must not be shared as a self-assessment starter pack. Anyone using the workflow for their own assessment needs a separate, clean project copy with their own evidence. Never present Philip's records, scores, examples, or conclusions as another person's evidence.

When helping a person assess their own evidence:

- Establish which title or titles they want to explore: Engineering Technician (EngTech), Incorporated Engineer (IEng), and/or Chartered Engineer (CEng). Ask which licensed institution or professional body they intend to use where that affects the process. Do not assume a title from a job, qualification, or current membership.
- Verify the current Engineering Council standard and the chosen institution's guidance before giving live application advice. The retained [UK-SPEC v4.2 source note](evidence/sources/registration/uk-spec-fourth-edition-v4.2.md) is a navigation aid to the local PDF, not a guarantee it is still the applicable edition.
- Assess each selected title against its own full A–E wording. The shared letter headings do not make the criteria or expected level identical. Use the comparison table as an orientation tool, not as a shortcut or ranking formula.
- For each criterion, identify concrete examples and trace them to that person's source evidence. Record their role, decisions, engineering judgement, constraints, collaboration, outcome and learning. Keep documented facts, personal recollection, third-party statements, inference and draft wording distinct; state evidence gaps plainly.
- Keep qualification and underpinning-knowledge route assessment separate from the competence evidence mapping. Where equivalence or route eligibility is unclear, identify it as unresolved and direct the person to their chosen institution for advice.
- Do not invent a score, pass threshold, probability of success, or registration verdict. Any self-rating is the person's provisional reflection only; a professional review is conducted by the relevant licensed institution. Do not add up ratings or treat coverage as a prediction.
- Ask for missing material facts one question at a time. Never fill a gap with a typical or likely answer.
- Treat narrative craft as a presentation aid only. The [*Story in Engineering* source note](references/story-in-engineering-les-chambers-v4.0.md) may help make an evidence-supported account easier to follow, but it must not change facts, conceal uncertainty, manufacture conflict or inflate the applicant's role.

## Text encoding

- Read and write repository text files as UTF-8 explicitly. In Windows PowerShell, do not use default-encoding `Get-Content`/`Set-Content` or other default-encoding pipelines for files containing non-ASCII text; use `[System.IO.File]::ReadAllText(..., [System.Text.Encoding]::UTF8)` and `[System.IO.File]::WriteAllText(..., [System.Text.UTF8Encoding]::new($false))`, or the project-managed Python interpreter with `encoding="utf-8"`.
- After editing text containing non-ASCII characters, read it back as UTF-8 and verify the affected text. Check for common UTF-8 mojibake sequences, replacement characters, and unintended C1 control characters. Do not apply a file-wide encoding conversion unless the resulting content is checked.

- When creating or editing text files, retain the repository line-ending policy and avoid introducing mixed line endings or line-ending-only churn.
- Preserve line endings in binary files.
