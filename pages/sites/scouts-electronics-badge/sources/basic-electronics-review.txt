====== Review note: //Basic Electronics// ======

* **Reviewed:** 2026-11-06
* **Source file:** [[https://github.com/PhilipMcGaw/Scout-Electronics-Badge/blob/main/Source%20Documents/Basic%20Electronics.pdf|Basic Electronics.pdf]]
* **Length:** 73 PDF pages
* **Attribution shown in the PDF:** Colin Mitchell, Talking Electronics
* **PDF metadata date:** 2016-02-16

===== Review scope =====

The PDF text was extracted and compared with the Scouts booklet, Leaders’ Guide, and blog drafts. The PDF includes diagrams and photographs; these were not visually inspected. Some extracted text has awkward spacing, so the review focuses on the readable prose and headings. This note records an assessment of the supplied copy, not a technical endorsement of all its examples.

===== Useful coverage and overlap =====

The source explains circuit lines and junctions, batteries, current, voltage, resistance, resistors, LEDs, diodes, capacitors, transistors, logic gates, multimeter use, and basic project construction. Most of these topics already appear in the learner booklet, including resistor current limiting, component polarity, capacitor ratings, transistor switching, digital logic, and safe meter connections. The source does not add enough verified material to justify duplicating those sections.

Battery capacity, internal resistance, and voltage drop under load are recorded for a dedicated blog post. The source's LDR and voltage-divider material prompted a potential-divider section in the resistor post, which can use Philip’s existing [[https://philipmcgaw.com/potential-divider-calculator/|Potential Divider Calculator]] as an interactive example. Philip is more likely to use thermistors than LDRs, so the post's worked sensor example will use a thermistor; check its type and behaviour against the selected part's datasheet rather than treating that as content from this PDF. The source also has useful background on resistor power and heating; the resistor post should explain the heat and rating relationship as a safety warning, not reproduce the source's deliberate resistor-heating demonstration.

The source includes mains-related circuit material. Do not adapt its mains-derived power-supply circuits or mains demonstrations into the booklet or blog drafts. Keep any brief warning that tells beginners not to work on mains equipment, but do not teach mains construction or repair here.

One useful editorial check was that the booklet said UK utility AC was 60 Hz in both its frequency explanations. Those statements have been corrected to describe the UK electricity network’s nominal 50 Hz frequency. The [[https://www.neso.energy/energy-101/electricity-explained/how-do-we-balance-grid/what-frequency|National Energy System Operator’s frequency explainer]] supports this UK-specific correction.

===== Material not adopted =====

The PDF is an older web-based resource and includes specific component values, circuit examples, and equipment recommendations. Do not copy its LED voltage figures, circuit values, build steps, or safety guidance without checking them against the actual parts, manufacturer datasheets, current UK guidance, and the planned activity. In particular, do not reproduce the demonstration that overloads a low-value resistor from a battery and invites the reader to feel the heat; explain resistor heating with a calculation and the component's power rating instead. The diagrams have not been visually checked, and none have been reproduced.

===== Disposition =====

The booklet’s UK mains-frequency wording was corrected. Battery behaviour is recorded for a dedicated blog post; a thermistor voltage-divider example is planned for the resistor post, based on Philip’s preference and to be checked against a component datasheet. The resistor demonstration is recorded as a warning to adapt safely. Mains-derived circuits and demonstrations are out of scope. This review note is not a substitute for the source document.
