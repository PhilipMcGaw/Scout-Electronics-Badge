===== Limits and editorial checks =====

* The first six supplied questions from an eight-question capacitor knowledge check have correct answers. The first selects “electric” for the field between differently charged plates, consistent with the capacitor-energy explanation. The second selects picofarads for the common three-digit capacitor marking convention; qualify this as a common code rather than a universal marking rule. The third selects electrolytic capacitors as usually polarised; note that non-polarised electrolytic types also exist and check exact markings. The fourth correctly states that ideal capacitances add in parallel; component ratings and non-ideal behaviour still need consideration. The fifth gives “twice” as the course’s intended voltage-rating margin; record this as a rule of thumb only and check the exact part and derating conditions. The sixth gives 63 % for a simple RC charge-time question; specify that it is about 63.2 % of the total voltage change after one time constant in the initially uncharged step-response case. The other two quiz questions have not been reviewed.

* “Challenge: Camera flash” warns correctly that a camera flash can contain dangerously high voltage; do not dismantle one or adapt its high-voltage circuitry. For its 9 V model, 15 mA sets an ideal charging-resistance lower bound of 600 Ω (''R = V/I''); actual resistor choice must include supply maximum, tolerance, initial power and battery limits. With an ideal 9 V initial capacitor voltage and constant 2 Ω discharge load, the voltage at 2 ms is ''9e^(−2 ms/(2 Ω × C))''; staying at or above 3 V requires ''C ≥ 2 ms/(2 Ω × ln(3)) ≈ 910 µF''. This is an idealised calculation, not a build-ready incandescent-lamp specification: filament resistance changes with temperature, and capacitor tolerance/ESR plus wiring and switch resistance alter the pulse. The ideal initial load current is 4.5 A, so the 9 V model still presents a high-current pulse and is not automatically suitable for Scout construction. Keep Scout supplies at or below 12 V and do not imply that a low voltage removes all current or stored-energy risks.

* “Solution: Challenge: Camera flash” chooses a 680 Ω resistor and a 1,000 µF capacitor. The ideal initial charge current is 9 V ÷ 680 Ω ≈ 13.2 mA and the nominal initial resistor dissipation is 9²/680 ≈ 0.12 W. For a constant 2 Ω load, 1,000 µF gives a 2 ms time constant; the voltage after 2 ms is about 3.3 V and the ideal interval above 3 V is about 2.2 ms. This satisfies the stated targets in the simplified model, but it does not make a real bulb/capacitor build automatically suitable: check maximum supply and resistor tolerance/pulse conditions, filament resistance, capacitor tolerance and ESR, switch/wiring resistance, and repetition rate. Keep this as an adult calculation rather than a Scout build recommendation.

* “RC circuits” is a useful demonstration of charging and discharging a capacitor through a resistor while observing its voltage. The stated calculations are correct: 1 kΩ × 1,000 µF = 1 s, 1 kΩ × 10 µF = 0.01 s, and the initial current into an uncharged 1,000 µF capacitor from an ideal 5 V supply is 5 mA. For an ideal first-order RC step response, one time constant is 63.2 % of the total voltage change; after five time constants the capacitor is about 99.3 % of the way to its final voltage, not exactly at it. During discharge, 36.8 % of the initial voltage remains after one time constant. State that these are idealised values; source resistance, initial voltage, capacitor leakage, component tolerances and any connected load affect real measurements. Explain that RC names the resistor-capacitor network, with a voltage source shown as a separate circuit element. A balloon is only a limited analogy and must not imply a capacitor is filled with charge like air in a container. Keep any Scout version within the 12 V supply limit, and identify oscilloscope trigger/time-base controls by the exact instrument manual if giving operating steps.

* “Capacitors in parallel” correctly states that ideal capacitances add and gives a correct nominal example: 10 µF + 1 µF + 1 µF = 12 µF. The combined bank must remain within each part’s voltage rating and any required derating; the lowest-rated part limits the permissible bank voltage. Check ripple-current, temperature and transient conditions as well. The stated tolerance result is correct when all parts have the same relative tolerance (for example, each ±5 %); for mixed tolerances, add the individual capacitance minima and maxima to find the worst-case range. Do not assume measured values will have the same tolerance as the nominal value without checking all parts and the measurement conditions.

* “Capacitors in series” gives the reciprocal formula, but its worked example mislabels reciprocal capacitances as farads and calls their sum a very large capacitance. In microfarads, 1/(1/10 + 1/1 + 1/1) = 0.476 µF; the terms being added are reciprocal capacitances. Equal capacitors in series have one divided by their count of the individual capacitance. The instructor’s voltage-sharing explanation is an idealisation: real capacitor voltage division depends on capacitance, leakage and operating conditions, and some component types must not be connected in series. Do not imply that series connection alone provides a safe higher voltage rating. Check the exact manufacturer guidance and, where allowed, design voltage balancing for the parts and circuit. TDK’s [[https://www.tdk-electronics.tdk.com/download/185386/31a5416e653dd6e4e428b8208d65cc2e/pdf-generaltechnicalinformation.pdf|aluminium electrolytic technical guide]] discusses voltage equalisation and balancing; Panasonic’s [[https://eu.industrial.panasonic.com/sites/default/pidseu/files/downloads/files/id_almiec_e.pdf|aluminium electrolytic capacitor guide]] warns of unequal sharing and calls for a bleeder resistor across each capacitor. Scout circuit supplies must not exceed 12 V, so do not use series stacks as a learner voltage-boosting activity.

* “Capacitor characteristics” usefully introduces rated voltage, capacitance tolerance, leakage current and ESR. Do not present “choose twice the expected voltage” as a universal rule; it is the instructor’s rule of thumb, and real selection must check the exact part’s continuous and transient voltage limits, ripple and temperature conditions, plus effective capacitance under DC bias where relevant. The example’s 5 V supply and 10 V-rated capacitor can illustrate that heuristic but is not a general prescription. Tighter tolerance may cost more in some families, but cost is not a dependable proxy for quality. Reject the claim that leakage is usually only a few nanoamps: leakage varies greatly with technology, capacitance, voltage, temperature, time and storage history. Similarly, do not generalise numeric ESR values such as “under 1 Ω” for ceramics or “a few ohms” for electrolytics; check the specified frequency, temperature, ESR and ripple-current ratings. Leakage and ESR matter more in long-term charge storage, timing, ripple and heating applications. The exact datasheet and circuit requirements determine whether they are significant.

* “Read capacitor values” gives a useful example of a three-digit pF code: ''103'' represents 10,000 pF (10 nF), and ''334K'' represents 330,000 pF (330 nF) with ±10 % tolerance in the code assumed. Qualify this as one common marking system: ceramic capacitor body markings are not universal, many MLCCs are unmarked, and manufacturer part-number fields may encode rated capacitance rather than being printed on the component. Tolerance letters depend on the applicable code and product series; do not infer a ''Z'' tolerance when no letter is visible. For electrolytics, distinguish the printed rated voltage from a safe operating allowance and check rated voltage, polarity and conditions against the exact datasheet. Capacitance measurement with a DMM is optional and meter-dependent; power off, safely discharge and isolate the part when circuit paths could affect the reading, then follow the meter manual. The course’s product examples and instruments are not UK purchasing recommendations.

* “Types of capacitors” is useful for comparing common packages and introducing polarity, markings and failure risks. Qualify the taxonomy: ceramic and electrolytic are common families, but film and other types also matter; their capacitance ranges overlap and are not fixed boundaries. Ceramic capacitors are generally non-polarised, but their capacitance stability depends on dielectric and operating conditions. Many aluminium and tantalum electrolytic capacitors are polarised, while non-polarised electrolytic types also exist. Show how to identify polarity from the exact part’s marking and datasheet; lead length is only a clue on some new leaded parts, not a reliable general rule. For a standard polarised aluminium electrolytic, reverse voltage can cause heating, gas generation, leakage, venting or failure; do not promise every part will explode or become a short circuit. Use the selected capacitor within its rated voltage, polarity, ripple and temperature conditions. Reject the transcript’s screwdriver shorting advice: directly shorting a charged capacitor can cause a high-current surge, sparks, tool/component damage or injury. In Scout activities, use only a Leader-approved, appropriately rated discharge method and verify the voltage with a meter before handling or storing a capacitor that may retain charge. Keep activities within the project’s 12 V maximum supply rule. The instructor jokes about released “magic smoke”; the user also calls it “factory smoke”. If that humour is retained, make clear it is a joke and that smoke, venting or leakage means a component has failed and needs safe assessment. If a schematic is included, use the project’s preferred IEC symbol style and make polarity clear.
* **User preference:** Philip prefers discharging capacitors through a resistor, typically around 1 kΩ. Treat this as a starting point, not a universal value; check the circuit voltage, capacitance, initial resistor power, pulse-energy rating and discharge time, then verify the remaining voltage.

* “What is a capacitor?” offers a beginner sequence from charge and capacitance to capacitor construction, stored energy, comparison with batteries, symbols and units. Keep the useful core: a capacitor has two conductive electrodes separated by a dielectric, and applied voltage separates charge on the electrodes while energy is stored in the electric field. Avoid describing electrons as passing through the dielectric or treating a capacitor as a balloon containing electrons. Current flows in the external circuit while the capacitor voltage changes; for an ideal capacitor connected to steady DC, that current falls to zero once charged, while a real part has leakage. A disconnected capacitor may retain charge, so do not assume it is safe to touch; use a suitable discharge method and verify before handling. The comparison that batteries have more energy density than capacitors is a useful broad contrast for ordinary capacitors, but is not a universal same-size rule covering supercapacitors and all battery technologies. The claimed usual range of a few picofarads to microfarads is not a design boundary: capacitance spans much wider ranges, including farad-scale supercapacitors. The instructor’s balloon demonstration is an analogy only and should not be repeated as a physical model.

* All six questions from the oscilloscope quiz are now reviewed. They cover voltage on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal axis, horizontal controls shared across channels, vertical controls adjusted per channel, Auto trigger mode updating without a trigger event, and the display plotting time against voltage. Clarify that divisions are grid intervals, while V/div and s/div specify the quantity represented by each division. * “Oscilloscope trigger settings” introduces edge slope, trigger level, and auto, normal and single acquisition modes. For an edge trigger, the scope uses a selected signal crossing a threshold on a rising or falling edge to align or initiate an acquisition; the trigger does not change the circuit signal. In normal mode, the display waits for a qualifying event; auto mode can continue updating without one, so an unsynchronised or wandering display does not prove that the signal is stable. Single mode waits for one qualifying event, captures an acquisition, then stops so the record can be inspected. Exact behaviour and names vary by scope. Distinguish trigger ''Auto'' mode from an ''Auto'' or ''Autoset'' button that changes display settings. Choose a trigger source with the signal, a slope that occurs, and a level within the signal’s range; jitter can come from signal variation, noise, or trigger setup, and should not automatically be attributed to one cause. * “Measure a signal” gives a useful optional workflow: connect a probe, match its attenuation setting to the scope channel, choose coupling, use a calibration output, then read voltage and time from the divisions. Correct the simplifications before reuse. ===== Separate quiz: questions 1–8 of 8 =====

* **Question 1:** Two resistors connected in series create a “voltage divider”. This is a useful shorthand, with an important circuit detail: the pair must be driven across the series combination and the output taken from the midpoint relative to one end.
* **Question 2:** Resistors connected in series have resistances that add together. This is correct: ''Rtotal = R1 + R2 + …''.
* **Question 3:** A photoresistor changes resistance with the amount of light on it. This is correct; an LDR's resistance generally falls as illumination increases, though the amount depends on the part and lighting conditions.
* **Question 4:** The answer to whether a voltage divider can convert a low voltage to a higher voltage is ''false''. A conventional passive divider between two supply rails produces an output between those rails; it cannot boost the input above its source rail.
* **Question 5:** Resistors connected in parallel have a combined resistance less than any individual branch resistance. This is correct for ordinary positive, finite resistor values.
* **Question 6:** A rheostat is the two-terminal variable-resistance device. A mechanical potentiometer can be wired in this rheostat configuration using its wiper and one end terminal; its three-terminal divider use is different.
* **Question 7:** A Wheatstone bridge consists of “parallel voltage dividers”. More precisely, its two series-divider branches are connected in parallel across the excitation supply, and the bridge output is measured between their midpoints.
* **Question 8:** The total current entering a circuit node equals the total current leaving it. This is true and states Kirchhoff's Current Law.

===== “Resistors in series” excerpt =====

The lesson correctly explains that series resistance is the sum of the individual values, that the same current flows through every series part, and that resistor dissipation can be calculated with ''P = I² × R''. Three 1 kΩ resistors each at ±5% have a nominal total of 3 kΩ and a worst-case range of 2.85–3.15 kΩ. Its claim that the resulting string simply has the same tolerance is valid for this example because all resistors have the same percentage tolerance; for unequal tolerances, calculate the total worst-case bounds from each part’s own limits. The lesson’s equal-resistor example at 20 mA requires 60 V across 3 kΩ, so do not transfer it into Scout activities, which are limited to 12 V DC. The “weakest link” description of string power rating is too simple: calculate each resistor’s power and voltage stress, apply the exact part’s derating and thermal conditions, and determine which limit constrains the string. For the article, use a low-voltage example and do not claim that power ratings always add or that equal values are always best.

===== “Resistors in parallel” excerpt =====

The lesson correctly states that parallel resistors have the same voltage across each branch, their branch currents add, and equivalent resistance is below the smallest branch resistance. The user-supplied diagram frame shows each resistor connected across the same two rails and agrees with the reciprocal formula. For identical resistors, ''Rtotal = R ÷ n''; for different values, use ''1 ÷ Rtotal = 1 ÷ R1 + 1 ÷ R2 + …''. The reciprocal terms are conductances measured in siemens (S), not ohms. The 10 kΩ and 20 kΩ example gives ''Rtotal ≈ 6.67 kΩ''; at 5 V, branch currents are 0.50 mA and 0.25 mA, and powers are 2.5 mW and 1.25 mW, respectively. Their sums give 0.75 mA and 3.75 mW. These are illustrative course values, not component recommendations. A resistance check across the parallel pair must be made with the circuit de-energised; otherwise the meter reading can be invalid and the meter may be damaged. Do not reuse the course frame; draw any project diagram in the preferred IEC style and check the crossing and junction conventions.

===== “Kirchhoff's circuit laws” excerpt =====

The transcript introduces Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) as equality between total current entering and leaving a connected node, and Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) as a zero directed sum of voltage rises and drops around a closed loop. Its ''IA + IB = IC + ID'' example and signed voltage-loop method are suitable teaching patterns when the diagram and reference directions are clear. Explain that current and voltage reference arrows are choices for analysis; a negative result means the actual direction or polarity is opposite to the chosen reference. Use IEC symbols and the project's dot/jump-over convention so a crossing is not mistaken for a junction. Keep the source's 9 V loop as a mathematical illustration only; use a checked low-voltage Scout example if adapting it to a practical activity.

===== “Wheatstone Bridge” excerpt =====

The bridge is best described as two series voltage dividers in parallel across an excitation supply; measure the differential output between their midpoints. With R1 above R2 on the left and R3 above Rx on the right, ''VL = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)'', ''VR = Vin × Rx ÷ (R3 + Rx)'', and the transcript defines ''Vout = VR − VL''. At balance, the divider outputs match, giving ''R1 ÷ R2 = R3 ÷ Rx'' and ''Rx = R2 × R3 ÷ R1''. The transcript's example reports Vout = −2.3 V for a 5 V excitation with all three known resistors at 10 kΩ and Rx adjusted below 1 kΩ. This sign is consistent with the stated convention: VL = 2.5 V and VR = 0.2 V, so Rx = 10 kΩ × 0.2 ÷ 4.8 ≈ 417 Ω. If substituting into a signed unbalanced-bridge formula, preserve the negative sign; using +2.3 V would imply a different Rx and contradict the stated setup. The transcript's claim of “extremely accurate” measurement needs qualification: bridge balance does not remove resistor tolerance, temperature effects, wiring/contact resistance, excitation error, meter loading, or calibration needs. A differential measurement is taken between the two midpoint nodes, not from either one to ground. Use IEC-style original diagrams if needed and keep Scout practice within 12 V.

===== “Challenge: Light activated switch” excerpt =====

The challenge transcript specifies that its relay input turns on when ''A > B'' and off when ''A ≤ B'', so a light-detector design intended to activate in bright conditions must produce ''A > B'' in light and ''A ≤ B'' in darkness. Its stated LDR values (about 1 kΩ in light and 10 kΩ in darkness) are course examples, not guaranteed sensor limits; check the selected LDR under the intended lighting. This transcript resolves the ambiguity recorded from worksheet 03_09: that sheet's dark-state condition ''B ≤ A'' is ''A ≥ B'', which overlaps the stated light condition instead of describing the relay's off state. The relay is an abstract interface here: the transcript gives no input thresholds, current limits, common-mode range, or isolation details. Do not connect a Scout-built detector to an unspecified relay or stereo, and keep mains equipment outside the activity. With the bridge powered from a checked low-voltage supply, use a DMM in voltage mode across A and B to check its differential output before connecting any allowed, fully specified low-voltage interface.

===== “Solution: Challenge: Light activated switch” excerpt =====

The solution chooses equal 10 kΩ resistors in the left divider, so with a 5 V excitation its midpoint A is 2.5 V. On the right, it places the adjustable resistor above the LDR and sets the top resistance to match the LDR at the desired light threshold; at 5 kΩ each, midpoint B is also 2.5 V. With the course's illustrative 1 kΩ bright and 10 kΩ dark LDR values, the ideal divider outputs are B ≈ 0.83 V in bright light and B ≈ 3.33 V in darkness. The solution defines the differential output as ''VA − VB'' (left/A positive, right/B negative), so it is positive in bright light and negative in darkness. This is the opposite polarity to the earlier “Wheatstone Bridge” excerpt's stated ''VR − VL'' convention; both are valid sign choices, but output terminals and equations must agree. The arithmetic assumes an unloaded bridge and exact resistor values; the instructor reports shadow/flashlight checks but these are not independent measurements. A real LDR's response depends on the part and illumination, and the bridge only creates a sensor signal: do not infer compatibility with an unspecified relay from the demonstration.

===== “Voltage dividers” excerpt =====

The lesson’s 3 V divider example is consistent: 1 kΩ and 2 kΩ in series draw 1 mA when unloaded, leaving 2 V across the lower 2 kΩ resistor. For the output across R2, use ''Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)''. One sentence in the transcript says R1 in the numerator, but the user-supplied diagram and the transcript’s later ratio correctly show R2; treat the spoken wording as a slip. Say explicitly that this formula assumes the output is unloaded. A device connected across the output is in parallel with R2 and changes the divider ratio; model or measure that loading before relying on the output. The course’s traffic analogy can help introduce voltage drops, but keep the actual explanation anchored in current, resistance and measured voltage. Do not reuse the supplied course frame without established image rights.

===== “Shift voltage levels” excerpt =====

The transcript uses a resistor divider as a possible way to reduce a one-way logic signal, but its 5 V Arduino/3.3 V accelerometer example is not universal advice. A real design must check the exact board pin’s output level and current capability, the destination input’s absolute maximum and logic thresholds, the signal speed and capacitance, and whether the bus is unidirectional or bidirectional. An ordinary divider can load a signal and is not a general solution for bidirectional interfaces such as I²C. The suggested 1–10 kΩ range is a starting point, not a universal rule; calculate divider current and loading for the exact design. A divider should not provide a device’s supply voltage: for a stated 400 mA load dropping 12 V to 5 V, the top resistor alone would dissipate at least 2.8 W, before accounting for divider current, and the load would change the output ratio. Use a suitable regulator or adapter for power, not a resistor divider. Keep Scout circuit supplies within the project’s 12 V DC limit; do not adapt the transcript’s wall-adapter example into a Scout activity.

===== “Measure resistive sensors” excerpt =====

The lesson correctly explains that a microcontroller analogue-to-digital converter measures voltage, so a resistive sensor can be placed in a potential divider and its resistance inferred from the measured output. With known R1 above the output and sensor R2 below it, rearranging the unloaded divider formula gives ''R2 = Vout × R1 ÷ (Vin − Vout)''. The transcript's LDR resistance examples and observed voltage readings are specific to its component, lighting, circuit and Arduino setup; do not present them as general LDR specifications or expected readings. Any practical example must state divider orientation and verify the selected sensor, supply/reference, ADC resolution and input limits. Measurements close to either rail are sensitive to ADC error and can make the resistance calculation unstable; use a suitable divider range and handle endpoint readings explicitly. Prefer a thermistor for the main project example, reflecting Philip's stated practice, while explaining that its resistance-temperature curve and NTC/PTC behaviour depend on the exact part. The course's Arduino code is not included in the supplied transcript and should not be inferred.

===== “Potentiometers” excerpt =====

The lesson describes a mechanical potentiometer as a three-terminal adjustable divider: the end terminals connect to the resistive element and the wiper is the adjustable output. It can be used as a two-terminal variable resistor (rheostat) using the wiper and one end terminal. The transcript's 20 kΩ slider, 10 kΩ trimpot and Arduino 5 V ADC example are component and board examples, not universal recommendations. For an ADC input, confirm the pinout and keep the wiper voltage within the selected microcontroller's input limits across the full adjustment range. A rheostat used to control load current must be selected for its current, power dissipation and other ratings; the course's lamp example does not establish that any potentiometer can control any lamp. Check the exact part datasheet for terminal arrangement and adjustment direction. The Arduino code is not included in the supplied transcript.

===== “Resistor characteristics” excerpt =====

The lesson gives a useful checklist for selecting a resistor from its exact datasheet: nominal resistance, tolerance, operating temperature, power rating, maximum working voltage, package dimensions, and power derating. It also notes that datasheets vary in layout. The booklet adopts this as practical guidance and treats working voltage and power as separate limits; manufacturer datasheets may specify pulse or overload ratings only for defined conditions.

Two transcript claims need correction or qualification. Carbon film and carbon composition are separate resistor technologies, not alternate names for the same type; see Vishay’s [[https://www.vishay.com/docs/28771/basics.pdf|Basics of Linear Fixed Resistors]]. Do not teach the transcript’s approximate 250 kΩ dividing line as a general rule for which rating will limit a resistor. Vishay defines a critical resistance in relation to rated power and maximum working voltage and gives device-specific derating terminology in its [[https://www.vishay.com/docs/49873/49873_sg2113.pdf|resistor terminology guide]]. Check the exact part datasheet instead.

===== “Read resistor color codes” excerpt =====

The lesson gives a useful sequence for decoding four-band and five-band through-hole resistors: significant digits, multiplier, then tolerance. A wider gap can help orient a banded resistor, but readers should confirm the band scheme with a suitable chart. Do not say that five bands guarantee a particular precision or that four-band tolerance is nearly always gold or silver; band codes vary, and some six-band resistors use an extra band for temperature coefficient. Vishay’s [[https://www.vishay.com/docs/49411/resistor_color_code_calculator.pdf|resistor colour-code chart]] shows different commercial schemes; its [[https://www.vishay.com/docs/20020/smdmark.pdf|SMD marking document]] also illustrates that surface-mount markings depend on package and series. The booklet already has a basic colour-code explanation; expand it in the blog with verified examples and a tolerance-range calculation. Do not reuse the course chart without permission and attribution details.

===== “Measure resistance” excerpt =====

The lesson usefully connects resistor tolerance to checking a component with a digital multimeter, and explains why a resistor measured in circuit may read lower because other paths are in parallel. Keep meter instructions model-specific: terminal labels, resistance ranges, auto-ranging and over-range displays differ, so follow the meter’s own manual. A resistance measurement requires a de-energised circuit; discharge capacitors using the correct procedure, then remove the resistor or lift one lead when an in-circuit path could affect the result. Compare the measured value with the nominal value and tolerance, remembering that the meter has its own accuracy limits. Do not repeat the demonstration of measuring a person’s body resistance; it is not a useful component measurement. Fluke’s [[https://www.fluke.com/en-ie/learn/blog/digital-multimeters/how-to-measure-resistance|resistance measurement guide]] and [[https://assets.fluke.com/manuals/151718__umeng0000.pdf|DMM manual example]] support the power-off and manual-specific guidance.
