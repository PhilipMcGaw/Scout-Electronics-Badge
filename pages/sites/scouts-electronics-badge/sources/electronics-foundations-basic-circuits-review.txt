===== Limits and editorial checks =====

* All eight supplied questions from the capacitor knowledge check have correct answers in context. The first selects “electric” for the field between differently charged plates, consistent with the capacitor-energy explanation. The second selects picofarads for the common three-digit capacitor marking convention; qualify this as a common code rather than a universal marking rule. The third selects electrolytic capacitors as usually polarised; note that non-polarised electrolytic types also exist and check exact markings. The fourth correctly states that ideal capacitances add in parallel; component ratings and non-ideal behaviour still need consideration. The fifth gives “twice” as the course’s intended voltage-rating margin; record this as a rule of thumb only and check the exact part and derating conditions. The sixth gives 63 % for a simple RC charge-time question; specify that it is about 63.2 % of the total voltage change after one time constant in the initially uncharged step-response case. The seventh correctly compares the reciprocal-sum rule for capacitors in series with resistors in parallel. The eighth correctly identifies the resistor as limiting charging current in the simple RC circuit; actual current also depends on capacitor voltage, source and total circuit resistance.

* “Challenge: Camera flash” warns correctly that a camera flash can contain dangerously high voltage; do not dismantle one or adapt its high-voltage circuitry. For its 9 V model, 15 mA sets an ideal charging-resistance lower bound of 600 Ω (''R = V/I''); actual resistor choice must include supply maximum, tolerance, initial power and battery limits. With an ideal 9 V initial capacitor voltage and constant 2 Ω discharge load, the voltage at 2 ms is ''9e^(−2 ms/(2 Ω × C))''; staying at or above 3 V requires ''C ≥ 2 ms/(2 Ω × ln(3)) ≈ 910 µF''. This is an idealised calculation, not a build-ready incandescent-lamp specification: filament resistance changes with temperature, and capacitor tolerance/ESR plus wiring and switch resistance alter the pulse. The ideal initial load current is 4.5 A, so the 9 V model still presents a high-current pulse and is not automatically suitable for Scout construction. Keep Scout supplies at or below 12 V and do not imply that a low voltage removes all current or stored-energy risks.

* “Solution: Challenge: Camera flash” chooses a 680 Ω resistor and a 1,000 µF capacitor. The ideal initial charge current is 9 V ÷ 680 Ω ≈ 13.2 mA and the nominal initial resistor dissipation is 9²/680 ≈ 0.12 W. For a constant 2 Ω load, 1,000 µF gives a 2 ms time constant; the voltage after 2 ms is about 3.3 V and the ideal interval above 3 V is about 2.2 ms. This satisfies the stated targets in the simplified model, but it does not make a real bulb/capacitor build automatically suitable: check maximum supply and resistor tolerance/pulse conditions, filament resistance, capacitor tolerance and ESR, switch/wiring resistance, and repetition rate. Keep this as an adult calculation rather than a Scout build recommendation.

* “RC circuits” is a useful demonstration of charging and discharging a capacitor through a resistor while observing its voltage. The stated calculations are correct: 1 kΩ × 1,000 µF = 1 s, 1 kΩ × 10 µF = 0.01 s, and the initial current into an uncharged 1,000 µF capacitor from an ideal 5 V supply is 5 mA. For an ideal first-order RC step response, one time constant is 63.2 % of the total voltage change; after five time constants the capacitor is about 99.3 % of the way to its final voltage, not exactly at it. During discharge, 36.8 % of the initial voltage remains after one time constant. State that these are idealised values; source resistance, initial voltage, capacitor leakage, component tolerances and any connected load affect real measurements. Explain that RC names the resistor-capacitor network, with a voltage source shown as a separate circuit element. A balloon is only a limited analogy and must not imply a capacitor is filled with charge like air in a container. Keep any Scout version within the 12 V supply limit, and identify oscilloscope trigger/time-base controls by the exact instrument manual if giving operating steps.

* “Capacitors in parallel” correctly states that ideal capacitances add and gives a correct nominal example: 10 µF + 1 µF + 1 µF = 12 µF. The combined bank must remain within each part’s voltage rating and any required derating; the lowest-rated part limits the permissible bank voltage. Check ripple-current, temperature and transient conditions as well. The stated tolerance result is correct when all parts have the same relative tolerance (for example, each ±5 %); for mixed tolerances, add the individual capacitance minima and maxima to find the worst-case range. Do not assume measured values will have the same tolerance as the nominal value without checking all parts and the measurement conditions.

* “Capacitors in series” gives the reciprocal formula, but its worked example mislabels reciprocal capacitances as farads and calls their sum a very large capacitance. In microfarads, 1/(1/10 + 1/1 + 1/1) = 0.476 µF; the terms being added are reciprocal capacitances. Equal capacitors in series have one divided by their count of the individual capacitance. The instructor’s voltage-sharing explanation is an idealisation: real capacitor voltage division depends on capacitance, leakage and operating conditions, and some component types must not be connected in series. Do not imply that series connection alone provides a safe higher voltage rating. Check the exact manufacturer guidance and, where allowed, design voltage balancing for the parts and circuit. TDK’s [[https://www.tdk-electronics.tdk.com/download/185386/31a5416e653dd6e4e428b8208d65cc2e/pdf-generaltechnicalinformation.pdf|aluminium electrolytic technical guide]] discusses voltage equalisation and balancing; Panasonic’s [[https://eu.industrial.panasonic.com/sites/default/pidseu/files/downloads/files/id_almiec_e.pdf|aluminium electrolytic capacitor guide]] warns of unequal sharing and calls for a bleeder resistor across each capacitor. Scout circuit supplies must not exceed 12 V, so do not use series stacks as a learner voltage-boosting activity.

* “Capacitor characteristics” usefully introduces rated voltage, capacitance tolerance, leakage current and ESR. Do not present “choose twice the expected voltage” as a universal rule; it is the instructor’s rule of thumb, and real selection must check the exact part’s continuous and transient voltage limits, ripple and temperature conditions, plus effective capacitance under DC bias where relevant. The example’s 5 V supply and 10 V-rated capacitor can illustrate that heuristic but is not a general prescription. Tighter tolerance may cost more in some families, but cost is not a dependable proxy for quality. Reject the claim that leakage is usually only a few nanoamps: leakage varies greatly with technology, capacitance, voltage, temperature, time and storage history. Similarly, do not generalise numeric ESR values such as “under 1 Ω” for ceramics or “a few ohms” for electrolytics; check the specified frequency, temperature, ESR and ripple-current ratings. Leakage and ESR matter more in long-term charge storage, timing, ripple and heating applications. The exact datasheet and circuit requirements determine whether they are significant.

* “Read capacitor values” gives a useful example of a three-digit pF code: ''103'' represents 10,000 pF (10 nF), and ''334K'' represents 330,000 pF (330 nF) with ±10 % tolerance in the code assumed. Qualify this as one common marking system: ceramic capacitor body markings are not universal, many MLCCs are unmarked, and manufacturer part-number fields may encode rated capacitance rather than being printed on the component. Tolerance letters depend on the applicable code and product series; do not infer a ''Z'' tolerance when no letter is visible. For electrolytics, distinguish the printed rated voltage from a safe operating allowance and check rated voltage, polarity and conditions against the exact datasheet. Capacitance measurement with a DMM is optional and meter-dependent; power off, safely discharge and isolate the part when circuit paths could affect the reading, then follow the meter manual. The course’s product examples and instruments are not UK purchasing recommendations.

* “Types of capacitors” is useful for comparing common packages and introducing polarity, markings and failure risks. Qualify the taxonomy: ceramic and electrolytic are common families, but film and other types also matter; their capacitance ranges overlap and are not fixed boundaries. Ceramic capacitors are generally non-polarised, but their capacitance stability depends on dielectric and operating conditions. Many aluminium and tantalum electrolytic capacitors are polarised, while non-polarised electrolytic types also exist. Show how to identify polarity from the exact part’s marking and datasheet; lead length is only a clue on some new leaded parts, not a reliable general rule. For a standard polarised aluminium electrolytic, reverse voltage can cause heating, gas generation, leakage, venting or failure; do not promise every part will explode or become a short circuit. Use the selected capacitor within its rated voltage, polarity, ripple and temperature conditions. Reject the transcript’s screwdriver shorting advice: directly shorting a charged capacitor can cause a high-current surge, sparks, tool/component damage or injury. In Scout activities, use only a Leader-approved, appropriately rated discharge method and verify the voltage with a meter before handling or storing a capacitor that may retain charge. Keep activities within the project’s 12 V maximum supply rule. The instructor jokes about released “magic smoke”; the user also calls it “factory smoke”. If that humour is retained, make clear it is a joke and that smoke, venting or leakage means a component has failed and needs safe assessment. If a schematic is included, use the project’s preferred IEC symbol style and make polarity clear.
* **User preference:** Philip prefers discharging capacitors through a resistor, typically around 1 kΩ. Treat this as a starting point, not a universal value; check the circuit voltage, capacitance, initial resistor power, pulse-energy rating and discharge time, then verify the remaining voltage.

* “What is a capacitor?” offers a beginner sequence from charge and capacitance to capacitor construction, stored energy, comparison with batteries, symbols and units. Keep the useful core: a capacitor has two conductive electrodes separated by a dielectric, and applied voltage separates charge on the electrodes while energy is stored in the electric field. Avoid describing electrons as passing through the dielectric or treating a capacitor as a balloon containing electrons. Current flows in the external circuit while the capacitor voltage changes; for an ideal capacitor connected to steady DC, that current falls to zero once charged, while a real part has leakage. A disconnected capacitor may retain charge, so do not assume it is safe to touch; use a suitable discharge method and verify before handling. The comparison that batteries have more energy density than capacitors is a useful broad contrast for ordinary capacitors, but is not a universal same-size rule covering supercapacitors and all battery technologies. The claimed usual range of a few picofarads to microfarads is not a design boundary: capacitance spans much wider ranges, including farad-scale supercapacitors. The instructor’s balloon demonstration is an analogy only and should not be repeated as a physical model.

* All six questions from the oscilloscope quiz are now reviewed. They cover voltage on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal axis, horizontal controls shared across channels, vertical controls adjusted per channel, Auto trigger mode updating without a trigger event, and the display plotting time against voltage. Clarify that divisions are grid intervals, while V/div and s/div specify the quantity represented by each division. * “Oscilloscope trigger settings” introduces edge slope, trigger level, and auto, normal and single acquisition modes. For an edge trigger, the scope uses a selected signal crossing a threshold on a rising or falling edge to align or initiate an acquisition; the trigger does not change the circuit signal. In normal mode, the display waits for a qualifying event; auto mode can continue updating without one, so an unsynchronised or wandering display does not prove that the signal is stable. Single mode waits for one qualifying event, captures an acquisition, then stops so the record can be inspected. Exact behaviour and names vary by scope. Distinguish trigger ''Auto'' mode from an ''Auto'' or ''Autoset'' button that changes display settings. Choose a trigger source with the signal, a slope that occurs, and a level within the signal’s range; jitter can come from signal variation, noise, or trigger setup, and should not automatically be attributed to one cause. * “Measure a signal” gives a useful optional workflow: connect a probe, match its attenuation setting to the scope channel, choose coupling, use a calibration output, then read voltage and time from the divisions. Correct the simplifications before reuse. A passive 10X probe is a compensated attenuator, not just a series resistor; 1X and 10X differ in input loading, capacitance, bandwidth and voltage limits, so use the exact probe manual and select the mode appropriate to the circuit and signal. The scope channel’s probe-ratio setting must match the physical switch. ===== Separate quiz: questions 1–8 of 8 =====

* **Question 1:** Two resistors connected in series create a “voltage divider”. This is a useful shorthand, with an important circuit detail: the pair must be driven across the series combination and the output taken from the midpoint relative to one end.
* **Question 2:** Resistors connected in series have resistances that add together. This is correct: ''Rtotal = R1 + R2 + …''.
* **Question 3:** A photoresistor changes resistance with the amount of light on it. This is correct; an LDR's resistance generally falls as illumination increases, though the amount depends on the part and lighting conditions.
* **Question 4:** The answer to whether a voltage divider can convert a low voltage to a higher voltage is ''false''. A conventional passive divider between two supply rails produces an output between those rails; it cannot boost the input above its source rail.
* **Question 5:** Resistors connected in parallel have a combined resistance less than any individual branch resistance. This is correct for ordinary positive, finite resistor values.
* **Question 6:** A rheostat is the two-terminal variable-resistance device. A mechanical potentiometer can be wired in this rheostat configuration using its wiper and one end terminal; its three-terminal divider use is different.
* **Question 7:** A Wheatstone bridge consists of “parallel voltage dividers”. More precisely, its two series-divider branches are connected in parallel across the excitation supply, and the bridge output is measured between their midpoints.
* **Question 8:** The total current entering a circuit node equals the total current leaving it. This is true and states Kirchhoff's Current Law.

===== “Resistors in series” excerpt =====

The lesson correctly explains that series resistance is the sum of the individual values, that the same current flows through every series part, and that resistor dissipation can be calculated with ''P = I² × R''. Three 1 kΩ resistors each at ±5% have a nominal total of 3 kΩ and a worst-case range of 2.85–3.15 kΩ. Its claim that the resulting string simply has the same tolerance is valid for this example because all resistors have the same percentage tolerance; for unequal tolerances, calculate the total worst-case bounds from each part’s own limits. The lesson’s equal-resistor example at 20 mA requires 60 V across 3 kΩ, so do not transfer it into Scout activities, which are limited to 12 V DC. The “weakest link” description of string power rating is too simple: calculate each resistor’s power and voltage stress, apply the exact part’s derating and thermal conditions, and determine which limit constrains the string. For the article, use a low-voltage example and do not claim that power ratings always add or that equal values are always best.

===== “Resistors in parallel” excerpt =====

The lesson correctly states that parallel resistors have the same voltage across each branch, their branch currents add, and equivalent resistance is below the smallest branch resistance. The user-supplied diagram frame shows each resistor connected across the same two rails and agrees with the reciprocal formula. For identical resistors, ''Rtotal = R ÷ n''; for different values, use ''1 ÷ Rtotal = 1 ÷ R1 + 1 ÷ R2 + …''. The reciprocal terms are conductances measured in siemens (S), not ohms. The 10 kΩ and 20 kΩ example gives ''Rtotal ≈ 6.67 kΩ''; at 5 V, branch currents are 0.50 mA and 0.25 mA, and powers are 2.5 mW and 1.25 mW, respectively. Their sums give 0.75 mA and 3.75 mW. These are illustrative course values, not component recommendations. A resistance check across the parallel pair must be made with the circuit de-energised; otherwise the meter reading can be invalid and the meter may be damaged. Do not reuse the course frame; draw any project diagram in the preferred IEC style and check the crossing and junction conventions.

===== “Kirchhoff's circuit laws” excerpt =====

The transcript introduces Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) as equality between total current entering and leaving a connected node, and Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) as a zero directed sum of voltage rises and drops around a closed loop. Its ''IA + IB = IC + ID'' example and signed voltage-loop method are suitable teaching patterns when the diagram and reference directions are clear. Explain that current and voltage reference arrows are choices for analysis; a negative result means the actual direction or polarity is opposite to the chosen reference. Use IEC symbols and the project's dot/jump-over convention so a crossing is not mistaken for a junction. Keep the source's 9 V loop as a mathematical illustration only; use a checked low-voltage Scout example if adapting it to a practical activity.

===== “Wheatstone Bridge” excerpt =====

The bridge is best described as two series voltage dividers in parallel across an excitation supply; measure the differential output between their midpoints. With R1 above R2 on the left and R3 above Rx on the right, ''VL = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)'', ''VR = Vin × Rx ÷ (R3 + Rx)'', and the transcript defines ''Vout = VR − VL''. At balance, the divider outputs match, giving ''R1 ÷ R2 = R3 ÷ Rx'' and ''Rx = R2 × R3 ÷ R1''. The transcript's example reports Vout = −2.3 V for a 5 V excitation with all three known resistors at 10 kΩ and Rx adjusted below 1 kΩ. ===== “Sine wave characteristics” excerpt =====

This lesson provides useful introductions to periodic waveforms, amplitude, frequency, period and phase, but correct several terms and generalisations before adapting it. ===== “L/R response” excerpt =====

For a simple series RL circuit driven by an ideal voltage step, let ''R_total'' include the series resistor, inductor winding resistance, and source resistance. The time constant is ''τ = L/R_total''; current rises as ''i(t) = (V_s/R_total)(1 − e^(−t/τ))'', resistor voltage rises with it, and ideal inductor voltage decays as ''v_L(t) = V_s e^(−t/τ)''. At one time constant, current has completed 63.2% of its total change; after five time constants it is about 99.3% of its final value. On switch-off, current decays rather than stopping instantly, and the inductor can produce a substantial voltage as it opposes the change; do not omit appropriate switching protection from a practical design. Describe the initial inductor voltage as the source voltage in this ideal step model, not as infinite impedance. Measuring resistor voltage can infer series current through Ohm’s law when the resistance is known. Two oscilloscope channels do not automatically provide differential measurements: follow the exact scope/probe manual and keep ground clips at a safe, shared circuit reference. ===== “Impedance” excerpt =====

The lesson is a useful introduction to capacitive reactance, frequency-dependent impedance and phase, but correct several terms and idealisations before reuse. The term is **reactance** (not “reactants”). For a sinusoid at angular frequency ''ω = 2πf'', ideal capacitive reactance magnitude is ''X_C = 1/(ωC)'', measured in ohms; complex capacitor impedance is ''Z_C = 1/(jωC) = -jX_C''. Impedance is not generally the arithmetic sum of resistance and reactance magnitudes: in sinusoidal steady-state analysis, ''Z = R + jX'' for a series combination, with sign and phase retained. For an ideal capacitor, current leads voltage by 90°; saying capacitor voltage lags current is equivalent. Do not say current is permanently blocked by a real capacitor at DC: an ideal capacitor draws no steady-state DC after charging, but real parts have leakage, and a changing voltage produces current (''i = C dv/dt''). Likewise, the ideal ''X_C → 0'' high-frequency limit does not describe a real capacitor indefinitely; ESR and ESL produce a self-resonance and inductive behaviour above it. The course’s “similar to a resistor” analogy is only about impedance magnitude at one frequency and omits phase. A basic DMM resistance mode does not measure frequency-dependent impedance. Verify device-specific values and measurement methods against the exact component or instrument documentation.

The supplied course frame gives ''1/(2πfC)'' as ''Z_C'' for a 10 µF capacitor. Its example values are approximately 15.9 Ω at 1 kHz and 1.59 kΩ at 10 Hz (the displayed 1591 Ω is truncated; to the nearest ohm it is 1592 Ω). That formula gives ''|Z_C| = X_C'', the magnitude, not the complex ''Z_C''; label it accordingly. At 0 Hz, the expression has a zero denominator and is not directly evaluable; say its ideal limit tends to infinity as frequency tends to zero. Real capacitors still have leakage.

===== “Impedance matching and bridging” excerpt =====

The transcript usefully distinguishes a signal source’s output (source) impedance from a receiving circuit’s input impedance and models their connection as a voltage divider. For a resistive Thevenin source with open-circuit voltage ''V_s'', source resistance ''R_s'' and load ''R_L'', the load voltage is ''V_L = V_s × R_L/(R_s + R_L)''. A 10:1 load-to-source ratio gives about 90.9% of the open-circuit voltage; a 1000:1 ratio gives about 99.9%. These figures are correct for the stated simple resistive model. The “at least 10 times” criterion is a rule of thumb, not a universal definition or requirement, and a 100:1 or 1000:1 ratio is not always needed. Use the error tolerance and frequency-dependent impedances of the actual source and load to decide.

Keep **impedance bridging** (a relatively high input impedance to avoid loading a voltage source) distinct from **impedance matching**. For a resistive Thevenin source, equal source and load resistance maximises power delivered to the load, but also dissipates half the source’s available power in the source resistance; this is not normally the aim when passing a voltage signal between amplifier or filter stages. In an AC network with reactive impedances, maximum power transfer uses complex-conjugate matching. On a transmission line, matching the load to the line’s characteristic impedance reduces reflections. Do not imply that every lumped source/load mismatch necessarily creates a reflected travelling wave; reflections matter when the interconnect and signal timing make transmission-line effects relevant. Analog Devices’ [[https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/radio-frequency-rf-impedance-matching-calculations-and-simulations.html|RF matching tutorial]] covers conjugate matching, while the University of Colorado Boulder [[https://physicslabs.colorado.edu/courses/phys-3330/lab-guides/lab2/|voltage-divider measurement lab]] explains source impedance and loading.

===== “Logarithmic scale” excerpt =====

This lesson is a useful visual introduction to why logarithmic axes help display quantities spanning several orders of magnitude. On a logarithmic axis, equal distances represent equal ratios, not equal differences; a decade is a factor of 10 and an octave is a factor of 2. A semi-log graph has one logarithmic axis and a log-log graph has two. In the course’s filter example, frequency is shown on a logarithmic horizontal axis; do not confuse a logarithmic frequency axis with expressing the magnitude in decibels. A Bode magnitude plot often uses dB, while the phase plot usually uses a linear angular scale, and the frequency axis uses a logarithmic scale. Zero cannot appear on a logarithmic frequency axis. The building-height analogy is an illustration only; avoid adopting its US units or first-person claims.

===== “Decibels” excerpt =====

The lesson gives useful common conversions, but the blog should state the quantities and references explicitly. For a power ratio, ''G_dB = 10 log10(P₂/P₁)''. A voltage-level or transfer ratio is commonly written ''G_dB = 20 log10(V₂/V₁)''; it represents the same power ratio only when impedances are equal, otherwise use ''P = V²/R'' (or the appropriate impedance relation) to calculate the power ratio. A decibel is a logarithmic ratio, not an absolute level by itself. ''dBm'' references power to 1 mW; ''dBW'' references power to 1 W; ''dBV'' references voltage to 1 V (normally RMS for AC). Keep case exact. 0 dB means a ratio of 1; approximately +3 dB doubles power, while +6 dB doubles voltage amplitude and, for equal impedances, corresponds to about four times the power. The corresponding negative values halve power or voltage; −6 dB in voltage corresponds to one quarter the power at equal impedances. A zero ratio has no finite logarithm; ''−∞ dB'' is its limiting representation, while real measurements are bounded by a noise floor. Gains expressed in dB add across cascaded stages only when the ratios are consistently defined under the actual loading; do not add absolute dBm levels as if they were gains. Active amplifiers can deliver output power greater than the input signal by drawing energy from their supply. The course’s 0-to-90 dB example maps power ratios of 1 to 10⁹, not voltage ratios; do not generalise that mapping to amplitude.

For references, use MIT OpenCourseWare’s [[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-004-dynamics-and-control-ii-spring-2008/f8706caa7aecebcc533553fb154778d0_lecture_33.pdf|Lecture 33]] for power/amplitude ratio definitions, dBm/dBW/dBV examples and dB summation, and the US NTIA’s [[https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/tn_89-2.pdf|Spectrum Management Technical Handbook]] for RF conversions. Treat the NTIA handbook as a technical reference, not UK regulation.

The user-supplied course table is arithmetically consistent to its displayed precision: for example, +20 dB corresponds to power ratio 100 and voltage ratio 10; +6 dB is power ratio about 3.981 and voltage ratio about 2; −6 dB is about 0.251 power ratio and 0.501 voltage ratio. Interpret the voltage ratios as power-equivalent only for equal impedances. The rows also illustrate why a dB figure alone does not tell you the linear ratio unless you know whether it refers to power or voltage.

===== “RC low pass filter” excerpt =====

The lesson introduces a first-order passive RC low-pass: series resistance, shunt capacitance, and output measured across the capacitor. For the ideal source and unloaded output in this topology, ''f_c = 1/(2πRC)''. A 10 kΩ resistor and 10 nF capacitor give ''f_c ≈ 1591.5 Hz'', so the course’s “about 1,592 Hz” result is correct. Real component tolerances shift the corner, and source resistance or a load across the output changes the response; use the effective circuit values, not just the nominal R and C, when precision matters.

The cutoff is not a sharp pass/stop boundary. For this single-pole RC low-pass, the magnitude is ''1/√2'' (about 0.707, or −3.01 dB relative to the passband) at ''f_c'', and its phase is −45°. The −3 dB point corresponds to half power only when power is compared at equal impedances; the 0.707 voltage ratio is the relevant transfer result for this filter. Above cutoff, the asymptotic roll-off is about −20 dB per decade. Describe the output as phase-lagged; it is not generally a fixed time delay. The transcript’s 1 V peak-to-peak sine-wave sweep is a useful measurement idea, but exact displayed results depend on source settings, probe loading, output loading and component tolerances. Do not treat course traces as reproducible measurements without the exact circuit and setup.

For checked RC formulas and transfer-response examples, see the [[https://physicslabs.colorado.edu/courses/phys-3330/lab-guides/lab3/|University of Colorado Boulder Filters lab]].

===== “RC high pass filter” excerpt =====

The lesson uses the first-order RC high-pass with a series capacitor, resistor from the output node to circuit common, and output measured across the resistor. For an ideal source and unloaded output, its transfer function is ''H(jω) = jωRC/(1 + jωRC)'' and its cutoff frequency is ''f_c = 1/(2πRC)''. With 10 kΩ and 10 nF, the nominal cutoff is about 1.59 kHz. At cutoff, the output voltage amplitude is about 0.707 of its passband value (−3.01 dB), and the output leads the input by 45°. The half-power interpretation assumes equal impedances; loading and source resistance change the response and effective cutoff.

The circuit blocks a steady DC component after the charging transient, so it can be used for AC coupling. Avoid saying that it always centres any waveform at zero: the output’s DC reference depends on the circuit connection, and a changing offset creates a transient. The source’s sweep is a useful demonstration idea, but its oscilloscope traces are not reproducible results without the exact setup. The University of Colorado Boulder [[https://physicslabs.colorado.edu/courses/phys-3330/lab-guides/lab3/|Filters lab]] also covers the first-order high-pass response.

===== “Challenge: Filter audio” excerpt =====

The challenge asks for a passive, first-order low-pass between a low-impedance audio source and a high-input-impedance amplifier, with a nominal −3 dB corner of 150 Hz ±10 Hz. For a series resistor, shunt capacitor, source resistance ''R_s'', and resistive amplifier input ''R_L'', the loaded pole is ''f_c = 1/[2πC((R_s + R) || R_L)]''. The passband voltage ratio is ''R_L/(R_s + R + R_L)'', so “−3 dB at cutoff” describes the drop relative to that loaded passband, not necessarily the ratio to the open-circuit source voltage. This follows the same Thevenin and voltage-divider model discussed in the impedance lessons.

One illustrative E24 solution is ''R = 11 kΩ'' and ''C = 100 nF''. With an assumed ''R_s = 3 Ω'' and ''R_L = 1 MΩ'', the pole is about 146.2 Hz and passband gain is about 0.989; with an ideal source and no load it is about 144.7 Hz. Both are within the stated 140–160 Hz window, but actual source output impedance and amplifier input impedance must be checked. By comparison, 10 kΩ and 100 nF give about 159.2 Hz unloaded and about 160.7 Hz with the illustrative 3 Ω/1 MΩ model, just outside the stated upper limit when loaded.

Explain that a first-order filter attenuates frequencies progressively at about 20 dB per decade; it does not pass everything below 150 Hz unchanged and reject everything above it. A single RC stage is a signal-level filter, not a complete loudspeaker crossover or a filter to place between a power amplifier and speaker. The course’s “only low frequencies” wording is a simplification; subwoofer crossover needs depend on the speakers, amplifier and system.

===== “Solution: Challenge: Filter audio” excerpt =====

The course chooses ''R = 10 kΩ'' and solves for ''C ≈ 106 nF'' at 150 Hz, then rounds to the available 100 nF. The exact nominal unloaded corner is ''1/(2π × 10 kΩ × 100 nF) ≈ 159.2 Hz'', which is within the allowed 140–160 Hz range. The solution assumes the amplifier input is several megohms: with a 3 Ω source and 3 MΩ load the loaded pole is about 159.6 Hz, also within range. If the actual amplifier input were 1 MΩ, the pole would instead be about 160.7 Hz, just outside the limit; check the real values rather than relying on “high impedance” alone.

At exactly 150 Hz, an ideal unloaded 159.2 Hz low-pass has a voltage ratio of about 0.728, while the reported oscilloscope observation is about 0.71. Treat the observed trace as an approximate course result, affected by component tolerances and setup, not a verified reproduction. A 0.71 voltage ratio is close to −3 dB; describe this as an amplitude reduction, and only equate −3 dB with half the power when the relevant impedances are equal. The music comparison is illustrative and does not establish a crossover response or performance with a specific subwoofer and amplifier.

===== Knowledge check: “Impedance matching and bridging”, questions 1–15 of 15 =====

Question 1 asks which load impedance maximises signal voltage transfer between two circuits. The supplied correct answer, **significantly greater than** the source impedance, is right for voltage bridging: in a Thevenin divider, increasing ''Z_L'' relative to ''Z_s'' reduces loading and makes the load voltage approach the open-circuit source voltage.

Question 2 asks which load impedance maximises power transfer. The supplied correct answer, **equal to** the source impedance, is correct for a simple resistive source and load. For complex AC impedances, maximum average power transfer requires the load to be the complex conjugate of the source impedance. These are different design aims: high load impedance favours voltage transfer, while matching maximises power under the applicable model.

Question 3 asks what bypass capacitors remove from DC supply lines. The intended answer is **AC signals**; more precisely, they can shunt some unwanted AC ripple or noise, depending on their impedance, placement and the supply/return path. Do not imply that a capacitor removes every AC component or that one value works in every circuit.

Question 4 asks for output amplitude at a filter’s −3 dB cutoff. The intended answer is **71%** (about 70.7%). This is the amplitude relative to the reference/passband level; it is the input amplitude only when the passband voltage gain is unity. It corresponds to half power only where the compared input and output impedances are equal.

Question 5 states that high-pass and low-pass RC filters can use the same components. The correct answer is **true**: a first-order RC version of each uses one resistor and one capacitor, with their positions and the output node arranged differently.

Question 6 gives **low** as the answer for the frequency energy blocked by a capacitor in an RC filter. The wording is ambiguous across both topologies: in a high-pass filter, a series capacitor impedes low frequencies; in a low-pass filter, a shunt capacitor diverts high-frequency components from the output. State which topology is meant rather than assigning one blocking role to every RC filter capacitor.

Question 7 asks which frequency components a low-pass filter attenuates. The correct answer is **high frequencies**. A real first-order filter attenuates them progressively; it does not eliminate every component above the cutoff.

Question 8 asks for output signal power at a filter’s −3 dB cutoff. The intended answer is **50%** of the reference power. The −3 dB point is half power relative to the passband (or maximum transmitted power); interpreting it as output power being half the input power requires equal impedances and a unity passband reference.

Question 9 states that impedance is measured in farads. The correct answer is **false**: impedance is measured in ohms (Ω); the farad (F) is the unit of capacitance.

Question 10 asks what the impedance of a component or circuit is the sum of. The correct answer is **resistance and reactance**. In sinusoidal steady-state AC analysis, impedance is the complex combination ''Z = R + jX'', where ''R'' is resistance and ''X'' is reactance; it is not generally a plain scalar sum of their magnitudes.

Question 11 asks what a Bode plot represents across frequency. The correct answer is **gain and phase shift**: conventionally, Bode plots show magnitude (often expressed as gain in decibels) and phase versus frequency, usually on a logarithmic frequency axis.

Question 12 states that capacitors have low impedance to **high-frequency** signals. In the capacitive operating range, ''|Z_C| = 1/(2πfC)'', so impedance falls as frequency rises. Real capacitors also have parasitic resistance and inductance, so this trend does not continue indefinitely at very high frequencies.

Question 13 asks how the major intervals on a logarithmic scale relate. The correct answer is **orders of magnitude**: equal distances on a logarithmic axis represent equal ratios, such as each decade spanning a factor of ten.

Question 14 states that a logarithmic scale is useful for displaying very large and very small quantities on one plot. The supplied answer, **true**, is correct: logarithmic axes can span many orders of magnitude, with equal distances representing equal ratios.

Question 15 gives **plus 10 dB** for a tenfold gain ratio. This is correct when the ratio is a power ratio: ''10 log₁₀(10) = 10 dB''. If “gain ratio” means a tenfold voltage or amplitude ratio, the result is +20 dB using ''20 log₁₀(10)'', assuming equal impedances. State whether the ratio is power or amplitude when using decibels. All 15 questions in the quiz have now been supplied.

===== “Decoupling capacitors” excerpt =====

The lesson introduces local supply decoupling as a way to reduce the effect of changing current demand and unwanted supply variation, and it correctly points out that schematic grouping does not dictate physical placement. A nearby capacitor can provide a local transient-current path, but it does not simply remove every high-frequency disturbance: effectiveness depends on the capacitor’s real impedance, the supply and return path, layout, load and frequency. Thermal noise should not be presented as though it were necessarily eliminated by placing a shunt capacitor across the rail.

Treat 100 nF and 1 µF or 10 µF as course examples, not a universal recipe. Follow the exact IC and regulator datasheets and layout guidance for values, ratings, number and placement of capacitors. For example, TI’s [[https://www.ti.com/lit/an/swra640a/swra640a.pdf|CC13xx/CC26xx hardware configuration and PCB design guide]] calls for close placement and a short, direct ground return path; this is device-specific guidance, not a general distance rule. Avoid advising Scouts to scatter arbitrary capacitor values along breadboard rails.

===== “Measure AC voltage” excerpt =====

The RMS explanation is useful: ''V_RMS = sqrt(mean(v(t)^2))'' gives the equivalent DC voltage for the same average heating in a resistor. For a zero-centred sine wave, ''V_RMS = Vp/sqrt(2)''. At the UK/EU nominal supply of 230 V RMS, this is about 325 V peak (the course’s US example is 120 V RMS, about 170 V peak). A displayed AC magnitude does not communicate instantaneous polarity.
