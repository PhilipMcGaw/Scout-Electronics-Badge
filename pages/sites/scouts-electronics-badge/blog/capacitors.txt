====== Capacitors: markings, polarity, and charge ======

**Status:** Outline started; draft not written.

**Existing articles:** [[https://philipmcgaw.com/capacitors/|Capacitor Basics]] and [[https://philipmcgaw.com/capacitor-value-prefixes/|Capacitor value prefixes]].

**Purpose:** Build on the concise existing post with practical ways to identify capacitors and understand what happens in a simple circuit.

===== Planned outline =====

- What capacitance means and how a capacitor differs from a battery and an inductor.
- Compare ceramic and electrolytic capacitors as common types, while noting film and other families. Their capacitance ranges overlap; do not assign a universal capacitance cutoff to a technology. Explain that ceramic capacitors are generally non-polarised and many electrolytics are polarised, but non-polarised electrolytic types exist.
- Use an indicative capacitance-versus-voltage chart to show that technology envelopes overlap, not as fixed limits or a substitute for checking a specific part datasheet. Distinguish single supercapacitor cell voltage from higher-voltage series modules. Prefer an original, independently checked graphic; do not reuse the EPCI image unless its image-specific licence or permission is confirmed.
- Show common forms (disc, SMD and can-shaped packages) only as examples. Identify polarity from the exact capacitor markings and datasheet; do not rely on colour or lead length alone. For a standard polarised electrolytic, explain why the positive terminal must remain at the correct potential and why reverse voltage can cause heat, gas, venting or failure.
- Use the project’s preferred IEC schematic symbols and mark polarity clearly; do not reproduce the course symbol artwork.
- Explain the two conductive electrodes and dielectric, separated charge and electric field; introduce ''Q = C × V'' and stored energy ''E = ½ C V²'' with a simple checked example. Clarify that current flows in the external circuit as capacitor voltage changes, not through the dielectric, and that real capacitors have leakage.
- Compare capacitors with batteries carefully: ordinary capacitors can charge and discharge quickly but usually store less energy per mass or volume; supercapacitors occupy a different range, so avoid a universal same-size claim.
- Recognise common units and read typical ceramic-capacitor markings.
- Decode common three-digit capacitance markings on parts that use them: the value is usually in pF, with the first two digits significant and the third giving the multiplier (for example, ''103'' = 10,000 pF = 10 nF). Present this as a common code, not a universal rule; many small MLCCs have no value printed on the body, and other packages use different markings. Use the manufacturer part number and datasheet when a marking is absent or ambiguous.
- Explain that tolerance letters follow a coding convention only within a specified marking system. For example, ''334K'' often denotes 330 nF ±10 % in the relevant code. Do not infer a tolerance from a missing letter or assume an unmarked capacitor has the widest tolerance; identify the actual part and check its datasheet.
- Show that ideal capacitances add in parallel: ''C_total = C1 + C2 + …''. For example, 10 µF + 1 µF + 1 µF = 12 µF nominal. Keep the combined rated working voltage within the rating and required derating of the lowest-rated part, and check ripple-current and temperature limits for the actual circuit.
- Explain tolerance using the limits of each part: total minimum and maximum capacitance are the sums of the individual minimum and maximum values. If all capacitors have the same percentage tolerance, the combined capacitance has that same percentage tolerance; mixed tolerances need a weighted calculation, not a blanket statement.
- Add a separate, brief section for adult/industry readers on safety-rated EMI suppression capacitors. Class X parts are intended for line-to-line positions where failure should not cause electric shock; Class Y parts are for line-to-earth or across-insulation positions where failure could cause shock. These are safety classes and circuit positions, not two capacitors connected in series. Some individual parts carry both X and Y approvals, but use them only in the positions and conditions covered by their markings and datasheet. Explain that IEC 60384-14 sets component requirements; LVD applies to finished electrical equipment, and using an approved capacitor alone does not make equipment compliant. For Great Britain, refer to the Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations 2016. Keep this as context only: no mains build or repair instructions, and do not include it in the Scouts booklet.
- Explain the ideal series rule: ''1/C_total = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + …''. Work the 10 µF, 1 µF and 1 µF example carefully: it gives about 0.476 µF, less than the smallest capacitor. For equal values, ''C_total = C/n''. Treat any increase in voltage capability as conditional: real capacitors may not share voltage evenly because of capacitance and leakage differences, and some types or manufacturers prohibit series use. Only claim a higher bank rating when the exact parts and circuit use an appropriate, manufacturer-approved voltage-balancing design. Keep Scouts’ circuit supply at or below 12 V; do not use this as a voltage-boosting activity.
- Read electrolytic markings as rated capacitance, rated voltage and polarity clues, not a promise that the part can tolerate any voltage up to that figure in any condition. Use it within its datasheet ratings; confirm the polarity mark for the exact package.
- Compare the circuit’s expected voltage with the capacitor’s rated working voltage. Apply any required derating and account for transients, ripple, temperature and DC-bias effects as specified for the exact part. The course’s “twice the expected voltage” is a rule of thumb, not a universal design rule; a higher voltage rating does not replace checking the effective capacitance and other ratings.
- Explain that leakage current is not a universal “few nanoamps”: it varies widely with capacitor type, capacitance, applied voltage, temperature, time and prior storage. Check the datasheet’s test conditions, especially when the capacitor must hold charge for a long time or set a timing interval.
- When discussing a capacitor’s value, distinguish nominal capacitance and tolerance from other selection data such as ESR, ripple-current rating, voltage rating, and capacitance change with DC bias, temperature and frequency. Explain ESR briefly where ripple current or losses matter, rather than presenting it as another unit of capacitance.
- Identify polarised and non-polarised parts; explain polarity and voltage rating, and what can happen if a polarised electrolytic capacitor is connected backwards.
- Show a low-voltage resistor-capacitor charge and discharge circuit. Explain that ''τ = R × C'' for this simple first-order circuit; after one time constant, the capacitor has completed about 63 % of the voltage change towards its final value, and after about five time constants it is near that value (about 99 %), not mathematically at it. On discharge, about 37 % of the starting voltage remains after one time constant. Explain that a larger R or C makes the response slower, and that the source, initial capacitor voltage and load affect the actual circuit response.
- If using the “Challenge: Camera flash” lesson as an optional worked design problem, clearly separate real camera flashes (dangerously high voltage; never dismantle one) from its 9 V model. For the ideal 9 V source and 15 mA maximum charge current, derive ''R ≥ V/I = 600 Ω'' as the theoretical minimum; choose a real preferred value using the supply’s maximum voltage and resistor tolerance, and check initial resistor power. For an ideal, fully charged 9 V capacitor discharging into a constant 2 Ω load, ''V(t) = 9e^(−t/(RC))'' gives a theoretical minimum ''C = 2 ms/(2 Ω × ln(3)) ≈ 910 µF'' to remain at or above 3 V for 2 ms. Do not present 910 µF as a build-ready value: bulb resistance changes with filament temperature, and capacitor tolerance, ESR, wiring and switch resistance affect the pulse. The ideal initial current is 4.5 A, so a low supply voltage does not make this a harmless Scout breadboard build. Keep the calculation as blog context, not a Scout activity; never exceed the Scout 12 V supply limit.
- Use a Falstad simulation to explore how changing resistance or capacitance changes the timing.
- Note practical limits: real components have tolerances and other non-ideal properties.

Use the course’s teaching progression as a conceptual guide: first show that a capacitor charges and discharges over time through a resistor, then explain that a transistor can use the changing capacitor voltage to switch another part of the circuit. Derive any example circuit, values and operating limits independently; do not copy the course’s 9 V kit diagrams or body-contact/pencil-probe activities. The separate exercise-file review records the RC examples and their limitations: [[sites:scouts-electronics-badge:sources:electronics-foundations-basic-circuits-exercises-review|Basic Circuits exercise-files review]].

===== Notes to carry forward =====

* The existing post covers capacitor uses, charge, energy, and the RC time constant.
* Use TDK’s [[https://www.tdk.com/en/tech-mag/electronics_primer/4|Capacitors Part 1: The Basics of Capacitors]] for a manufacturer explanation of electrode charge separation, dielectric insulation, external charging current, and the distinction between DC and changing signals. Write the explanation independently and check the actual component for its leakage and ratings.
* Do not imply that a capacitor contains a reservoir of electrons or that electrons cross its dielectric during normal operation. The balloon comparison is a limited analogy, not a physical description. A real disconnected capacitor can retain charge. Never recommend discharging it by touching the terminals with a screwdriver: use a resistor-based discharge method, then measure the remaining voltage before handling. Philip prefers about 1 kΩ as a starting point, but it is not a universal value: check initial current and resistor power (''P = V²/R''), pulse-energy rating and discharge time (''τ = R × C'') against the capacitor and circuit. Keep Scout circuit supplies at or below 12 V and have the Leader approve the setup.
* Review the value-prefix article before reusing its table. It lists 1 mF as 0.0001 F; the correct conversion is 0.001 F, and “millifarard” is a spelling error. It also presents electrolytic capacitors as more common above about 100 nF, which must not be treated as a technology boundary: ceramic and film types are also available at higher capacitances, as shown in [[https://www.kemet.com/en/us/capacitors.html|KEMET’s capacitor range]]. Recheck every prefix, marking, and technology comparison against current references before publication.
* Reader comments ask for clearer definitions of symbols and a comparison with battery and inductor energy storage.
* The course examples ''103'' = 10 nF and ''334K'' = 330 nF ±10 % have correct arithmetic for the code assumed. The marking convention and tolerance-letter meanings are not universal across all capacitor families and manufacturers. In particular, never assume a missing tolerance letter means the ''Z'' tolerance. Consult the exact series marking guide or datasheet.
* “RC circuits” provides a useful low-voltage demonstration sequence: close a switch to charge a 1,000 µF capacitor through 1 kΩ, then use a resistor path to discharge it and observe both voltage curves. The arithmetic is correct: ''τ = RC = 1 s'' for 1,000 µF and 1 kΩ, giving about 3.2 V after one second from an initially uncharged state with an ideal 5 V source; 10 µF with 1 kΩ gives 0.01 s. Clarify that an ideal capacitor approaches its final voltage asymptotically and is effectively, not exactly, settled after several time constants. Define the time constant as 63.2 % of the total voltage change for this simple step response; during discharge, 36.8 % of the initial voltage remains after one time constant. Describe the resistor-capacitor network as the RC circuit and show the source separately. The balloon analogy is limited: charge separation occurs on the electrodes, not by filling the capacitor with a substance. Scope trigger and time-base setup depend on the instrument and the event being captured.
* The first question supplied from the course’s eight-question capacitor quiz correctly identifies the electric field between oppositely charged plates as where a capacitor stores energy. Use it to reinforce the physical explanation that charge separation creates an electric field in the dielectric; avoid implying that energy is stored as a material “inside” one plate.
* The second supplied quiz question correctly gives picofarads as the unit for the common three-digit capacitor marking code. Qualify this as a convention used by many such markings, not a universal rule for every capacitor or part-number system; decode the exact component using its manufacturer documentation.
* The third supplied quiz question correctly identifies electrolytic capacitors as usually polarised. Keep the qualification in the outline: many electrolytics are polarised, but non-polarised electrolytic types exist. Check the exact component markings and datasheet rather than assuming polarity from the broad family name.
* The fourth supplied quiz question correctly states that capacitances add in parallel in the ideal model. Retain the practical qualifications already noted: voltage rating, tolerance, ESR and ripple-current limits still need checking for the actual parts and circuit.
* The fifth supplied quiz question gives “twice” as the course’s intended voltage-rating margin. Present that only as a rule of thumb, not a universal design requirement; select a capacitor using its exact datasheet and account for DC bias, transients, ripple, temperature and any other applicable derating.
* The sixth supplied quiz question correctly gives 63 % for the RC charging time constant in the simple case of an initially uncharged capacitor and a step voltage. State more precisely that after one time constant the capacitor has completed about 63.2 % of the total change towards its final voltage; for discharge, about 36.8 % of the starting voltage remains.
* The seventh supplied quiz question correctly compares capacitors in series with resistors in parallel: both use a reciprocal-sum relationship. For ideal capacitors in series, write ''1/C_total = Σ(1/C_i)'' and keep the series-capacitor voltage-sharing caveat; the analogy concerns the combination formula.
* The eighth supplied quiz question correctly identifies the resistor as limiting charging current in the course’s series RC circuit. Explain that the current also depends on the supply voltage, the capacitor’s instantaneous voltage and the total resistance in the real circuit; it falls as the capacitor charges.
* “Challenge: Camera flash” is suitable only as a calculation example. Never dismantle a camera flash: its capacitor can retain a dangerous high voltage. In the low-voltage model, 9 V ÷ 15 mA = 600 Ω is only an ideal minimum charge resistance; the chosen real resistor must account for maximum supply voltage and its tolerance, and its initial ''P = V²/R'' must be checked. For a constant 2 Ω load, the ideal discharge relation gives ''C ≥ 2 ms/(2 Ω × ln(9/3)) ≈ 910 µF'' to stay above 3 V for 2 ms from a 9 V initial charge. This is not a reliable component selection for an incandescent lamp without measuring its cold/hot resistance and accounting for capacitor tolerance, ESR and circuit resistance. The ideal starting load current is 4.5 A; do not turn the course challenge into a Scout build solely because its source is 9 V. Keep all Scout circuit supplies at or below 12 V.
* “Solution: Challenge: Camera flash” selects 680 Ω and 1,000 µF. At 9 V, the ideal initial charging current through 680 Ω is 13.2 mA; nominal initial resistor dissipation is about 0.12 W. With an ideal, constant 2 Ω load, 1,000 µF gives ''τ = RC = 2 ms'' and about 3.3 V remains after 2 ms; it stays above 3 V for about 2.2 ms in that model. These values meet the stated targets mathematically but are not guarantees for a real incandescent bulb or a capacitor with tolerance and ESR. Treat the demonstration result as source-specific, not as an endorsed Scout build.
* Treat capacitor tolerance, rated voltage, leakage current and ESR as different characteristics. Tighter tolerance may cost more in some product families but price is not a guaranteed proxy for quality. Leakage and ESR are especially dependent on construction and stated measurement conditions; do not reuse the transcript’s “few nanoamps”, “less than one ohm”, or “a few ohms” as general specifications. Check the exact datasheet.
* In an ideal parallel combination, capacitances and their charge capacities add: ''C_total = ΣCi''. The nominal 10 µF + 1 µF + 1 µF example gives 12 µF. The maximum safe working voltage of the group cannot exceed the lowest-rated capacitor and may need further derating for DC bias, ripple, temperature or transients. For tolerance, add individual worst-case minimums and maximums; the same relative tolerance on every part yields the same relative tolerance for the sum, but different tolerances require a weighted result.
* For ideal capacitors in series, ''1/C_total = Σ(1/Ci)''. The transcript’s 10 µF + 1 µF + 1 µF series example evaluates to about 0.476 µF, not a large number of farads: the sum of reciprocals is in inverse farads. Equal capacitors in series give ''C/n''. In the ideal model, series capacitors carry equal charge and divide voltage inversely with capacitance; real leakage, tolerance and frequency-dependent behaviour can cause unequal voltage sharing. Do not teach that putting capacitors in series automatically increases a safe voltage rating. Check whether the exact parts permit series operation; where applicable, a balancing network must be designed for leakage, voltage, power dissipation and transients. Panasonic’s guidance for aluminium electrolytics calls for a bleeder resistor across each capacitor, while manufacturers may prohibit series use for some capacitor types. Scout activities remain at or below 12 V supply.
* A DMM capacitance reading is an approximate check, not a substitute for the rated value and test conditions in the datasheet. Switch off, isolate and discharge the capacitor safely first; remove it from the circuit if parallel paths could affect the measurement, and follow the meter manual. The printed voltage is the rated working voltage under specified conditions, not an invitation to operate at or above it.
* Do not imply that all capacitors above or below a particular value use one construction or have the same polarity rules.
* Explain ESR (equivalent series resistance) as a simplified model of resistive losses inside a real capacitor. It varies with capacitor technology, frequency and temperature; it is not simply a fixed resistor added to the nominal capacitance. Ripple current flowing through ESR causes heat (approximately ''P = I² × ESR'', where ''I'' is the r.m.s. ripple current under the stated operating conditions), so check ESR and ripple-current ratings when selecting a part for smoothing, switching or other appreciable AC-current applications. Read the datasheet conditions for the ESR figure. Do not imply that choosing the lowest ESR is always best: replacing a capacitor can affect regulator stability and circuit behaviour, so follow the circuit and IC manufacturers’ guidance.
* Iain Sharp’s 2010 [[https://lushprojects.com/absolutebeginners/|Electronics for Absolute Beginners]] notes offer a simple RC charge/discharge activity followed by transistor-based timing circuits. The companion parts list includes 10 µF and 100 µF axial capacitors rated at 15 V or more, alongside ceramic capacitors from 470 pF to 100 nF; treat these as historical kit choices, not a recommended bill of materials. This sequence can help explain how a gradual capacitor-voltage change becomes a switching action. The notes’ broad shortcut that capacitors below 1 µF are usually non-polar and larger ones usually polarised is not a reliable selection rule. Identify polarity from the exact part and datasheet, and verify voltage, resistor current and capacitor discharge conditions before adapting any experiment. The notes are CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 Unported; do not reproduce their wording or figures without meeting the licence terms.
* A useful overview is All About Circuits’ [[https://www.allaboutcircuits.com/technical-articles/x7r-x5r-c0g...-a-concise-guide-to-ceramic-capacitor-types/|“X7R, X5R, C0G…: A Concise Guide to Ceramic Capacitor Types”]], but treat it as an introduction. X7R/X5R describe Class 2 temperature characteristics; C0G/NP0 is a Class 1 dielectric with much better temperature stability. Do not let the temperature code imply that the nominal capacitance stays constant under operating voltage: Class 2 MLCCs can lose substantial capacitance under DC bias, and can also age. Check the exact part’s datasheet at the actual bias and temperature.
* Class 2 ceramics can also produce or respond to mechanical vibration (often called piezoelectric or electrostrictive behaviour), which can make audible noise in some circuits. This is an application-dependent effect, not a reason to describe all X7R/X5R parts as unsuitable for audio.
* Keep the EIA temperature code explanation tied to its specified temperature range and capacitance-change class. Do not present it as a complete description of tolerance, DC-bias behaviour, ageing, voltage rating, or suitability for a circuit.
* Murata’s [[https://article.murata.com/en-us/article/temperature-characteristics-electrostatic-capacitance|temperature-characteristics guide]] is a useful manufacturer explanation of Class 1 temperature coefficients versus Class 2 capacitance-change ranges. It also distinguishes the temperature code from voltage-bias and ageing behaviour. Cite it alongside the actual part datasheet; its standard tables and examples may not cover every current regional standard or product.
* Explain that reverse voltage can heat and damage a standard polarised aluminium electrolytic, possibly causing gas pressure and venting; do not claim every reversed capacitor explodes or always fails short. Use the exact part datasheet and manufacturer warnings, check markings before power is applied, and allow any safety vent to remain unobstructed.
* If using the “magic smoke” joke, note that it is also called “factory smoke”. Keep it clearly humorous: smoke, venting, leaking electrolyte or a bulging case means switch off safely, do not touch the part, and have the Leader assess it. Never normalise component failure as part of a build.
* TDK’s [[https://www.tdk.com/en/tech-mag/electronics_primer/9|Dielectric Materials in Capacitors]] notes that electrolytic capacitors are normally polarised but non-polar types also exist. KEMET’s [[https://content.kemet.com/datasheets/KEM_A4026_ALC40.pdf|ALC40 datasheet]] describes reverse-voltage heating/gas generation and a safety vent for that particular product family; do not generalise its ratings or reverse-voltage allowance to other parts.
* Fluke advises turning power off, discharging through a resistor, and verifying the voltage before handling in its [[https://www.fluke.com/en/learn/blog/digital-multimeters/how-to-measure-capacitance|capacitance-measurement guide]]. Do not copy the course’s screwdriver method.
