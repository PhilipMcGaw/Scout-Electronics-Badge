====== Capacitors: markings, polarity, and charge ======

**Status:** Outline started; draft not written.

**Existing articles:** [[https://philipmcgaw.com/capacitors/|Capacitor Basics]] and [[https://philipmcgaw.com/capacitor-value-prefixes/|Capacitor value prefixes]].

**Purpose:** Build on the concise existing post with practical ways to identify capacitors and understand what happens in a simple circuit.

===== Planned outline =====

- What capacitance means and how a capacitor differs from a battery and an inductor.
- Compare ceramic and electrolytic capacitors as common types, while noting film and other families. Their capacitance ranges overlap; do not assign a universal capacitance cutoff to a technology. Explain that ceramic capacitors are generally non-polarised and many electrolytics are polarised, but non-polarised electrolytic types exist.
- Show common forms (disc, SMD and can-shaped packages) only as examples. Identify polarity from the exact capacitor markings and datasheet; do not rely on colour or lead length alone. For a standard polarised electrolytic, explain why the positive terminal must remain at the correct potential and why reverse voltage can cause heat, gas, venting or failure.
- Use the project’s preferred IEC schematic symbols and mark polarity clearly; do not reproduce the course symbol artwork.
- Explain the two conductive electrodes and dielectric, separated charge and electric field; introduce ''Q = C × V'' and stored energy ''E = ½ C V²'' with a simple checked example. Clarify that current flows in the external circuit as capacitor voltage changes, not through the dielectric, and that real capacitors have leakage.
- Compare capacitors with batteries carefully: ordinary capacitors can charge and discharge quickly but usually store less energy per mass or volume; supercapacitors occupy a different range, so avoid a universal same-size claim.
- Recognise common units and read typical ceramic-capacitor markings.
- When discussing a capacitor’s value, distinguish nominal capacitance and tolerance from other selection data such as ESR, ripple-current rating, voltage rating, and capacitance change with DC bias, temperature and frequency. Explain ESR briefly where ripple current or losses matter, rather than presenting it as another unit of capacitance.
- Identify polarised and non-polarised parts; explain polarity and voltage rating, and what can happen if a polarised electrolytic capacitor is connected backwards.
- Show a resistor-capacitor charge and discharge circuit, including the time constant.
- Use a Falstad simulation to explore how changing resistance or capacitance changes the timing.
- Note practical limits: real components have tolerances and other non-ideal properties.

Use the course’s teaching progression as a conceptual guide: first show that a capacitor charges and discharges over time through a resistor, then explain that a transistor can use the changing capacitor voltage to switch another part of the circuit. Derive any example circuit, values and operating limits independently; do not copy the course’s 9 V kit diagrams or body-contact/pencil-probe activities. The separate exercise-file review records the RC examples and their limitations: [[sites:scouts-electronics-badge:sources:electronics-foundations-basic-circuits-exercises-review|Basic Circuits exercise-files review]].

===== Notes to carry forward =====

* The existing post covers capacitor uses, charge, energy, and the RC time constant.
* Use TDK’s [[https://www.tdk.com/en/tech-mag/electronics_primer/4|Capacitors Part 1: The Basics of Capacitors]] for a manufacturer explanation of electrode charge separation, dielectric insulation, external charging current, and the distinction between DC and changing signals. Write the explanation independently and check the actual component for its leakage and ratings.
* Do not imply that a capacitor contains a reservoir of electrons or that electrons cross its dielectric during normal operation. The balloon comparison is a limited analogy, not a physical description. A real disconnected capacitor can retain charge. Never recommend discharging it by touching the terminals with a screwdriver: use a resistor-based discharge method, then measure the remaining voltage before handling. Philip prefers about 1 kΩ as a starting point, but it is not a universal value: check initial current and resistor power (''P = V²/R''), pulse-energy rating and discharge time (''τ = R × C'') against the capacitor and circuit. Keep Scout circuit supplies at or below 12 V and have the Leader approve the setup.
* Review the value-prefix article before reusing its table. It lists 1 mF as 0.0001 F; the correct conversion is 0.001 F, and “millifarard” is a spelling error. It also presents electrolytic capacitors as more common above about 100 nF, which must not be treated as a technology boundary: ceramic and film types are also available at higher capacitances, as shown in [[https://www.kemet.com/en/us/capacitors.html|KEMET’s capacitor range]]. Recheck every prefix, marking, and technology comparison against current references before publication.
* Reader comments ask for clearer definitions of symbols and a comparison with battery and inductor energy storage.
* Do not imply that all capacitors above or below a particular value use one construction or have the same polarity rules.
* Explain ESR (equivalent series resistance) as a simplified model of resistive losses inside a real capacitor. It varies with capacitor technology, frequency and temperature; it is not simply a fixed resistor added to the nominal capacitance. Ripple current flowing through ESR causes heat (approximately ''P = I² × ESR'', where ''I'' is the r.m.s. ripple current under the stated operating conditions), so check ESR and ripple-current ratings when selecting a part for smoothing, switching or other appreciable AC-current applications. Read the datasheet conditions for the ESR figure. Do not imply that choosing the lowest ESR is always best: replacing a capacitor can affect regulator stability and circuit behaviour, so follow the circuit and IC manufacturers’ guidance.
* Iain Sharp’s 2010 [[https://lushprojects.com/absolutebeginners/|Electronics for Absolute Beginners]] notes offer a simple RC charge/discharge activity followed by transistor-based timing circuits. The companion parts list includes 10 µF and 100 µF axial capacitors rated at 15 V or more, alongside ceramic capacitors from 470 pF to 100 nF; treat these as historical kit choices, not a recommended bill of materials. This sequence can help explain how a gradual capacitor-voltage change becomes a switching action. The notes’ broad shortcut that capacitors below 1 µF are usually non-polar and larger ones usually polarised is not a reliable selection rule. Identify polarity from the exact part and datasheet, and verify voltage, resistor current and capacitor discharge conditions before adapting any experiment. The notes are CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 Unported; do not reproduce their wording or figures without meeting the licence terms.
* A useful overview is All About Circuits’ [[https://www.allaboutcircuits.com/technical-articles/x7r-x5r-c0g...-a-concise-guide-to-ceramic-capacitor-types/|“X7R, X5R, C0G…: A Concise Guide to Ceramic Capacitor Types”]], but treat it as an introduction. X7R/X5R describe Class 2 temperature characteristics; C0G/NP0 is a Class 1 dielectric with much better temperature stability. Do not let the temperature code imply that the nominal capacitance stays constant under operating voltage: Class 2 MLCCs can lose substantial capacitance under DC bias, and can also age. Check the exact part’s datasheet at the actual bias and temperature.
* Class 2 ceramics can also produce or respond to mechanical vibration (often called piezoelectric or electrostrictive behaviour), which can make audible noise in some circuits. This is an application-dependent effect, not a reason to describe all X7R/X5R parts as unsuitable for audio.
* Keep the EIA temperature code explanation tied to its specified temperature range and capacitance-change class. Do not present it as a complete description of tolerance, DC-bias behaviour, ageing, voltage rating, or suitability for a circuit.
* Murata’s [[https://article.murata.com/en-us/article/temperature-characteristics-electrostatic-capacitance|temperature-characteristics guide]] is a useful manufacturer explanation of Class 1 temperature coefficients versus Class 2 capacitance-change ranges. It also distinguishes the temperature code from voltage-bias and ageing behaviour. Cite it alongside the actual part datasheet; its standard tables and examples may not cover every current regional standard or product.
* Explain that reverse voltage can heat and damage a standard polarised aluminium electrolytic, possibly causing gas pressure and venting; do not claim every reversed capacitor explodes or always fails short. Use the exact part datasheet and manufacturer warnings, check markings before power is applied, and allow any safety vent to remain unobstructed.
* If using the “magic smoke” joke, note that it is also called “factory smoke”. Keep it clearly humorous: smoke, venting, leaking electrolyte or a bulging case means switch off safely, do not touch the part, and have the Leader assess it. Never normalise component failure as part of a build.
* TDK’s [[https://www.tdk.com/en/tech-mag/electronics_primer/9|Dielectric Materials in Capacitors]] notes that electrolytic capacitors are normally polarised but non-polar types also exist. KEMET’s [[https://content.kemet.com/datasheets/KEM_A4026_ALC40.pdf|ALC40 datasheet]] describes reverse-voltage heating/gas generation and a safety vent for that particular product family; do not generalise its ratings or reverse-voltage allowance to other parts.
* Fluke advises turning power off, discharging through a resistor, and verifying the voltage before handling in its [[https://www.fluke.com/en/learn/blog/digital-multimeters/how-to-measure-capacitance|capacitance-measurement guide]]. Do not copy the course’s screwdriver method.
