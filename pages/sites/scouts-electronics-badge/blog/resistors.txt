====== Resistors: types, values, ratings, dividers, and practical use ======

**Status:** Outline started; draft not written.

**Existing articles:** [[https://philipmcgaw.com/learn-about-resistors/|Learn about Resistors]], [[https://philipmcgaw.com/ohms-law-and-watts-law/|Is P = I x V Part of Ohm’s Law?]], [[https://philipmcgaw.com/led-resistors-values/|LED Resistor Values]], and the [[https://philipmcgaw.com/potential-divider-calculator/|Potential Divider Calculator]].

**Purpose:** Refresh the existing article with clearer explanations and a practical example that shows how resistor choice affects a real circuit.

**New source material:** User-pasted transcripts “Challenge: Limit current to an LED”, “Solution: Limit current to an LED”, “Types of resistors”, “Resistor characteristics”, “Read resistor color codes”, “Measure resistance”, and “Voltage dividers”, plus course exercise sheets summarised in the [[sites:scouts-electronics-badge:sources:electronics-foundations-fundamentals-2021-review|2021 course materials review]] and [[sites:scouts-electronics-badge:sources:electronics-foundations-basic-circuits-exercises-review|Basic Circuits exercise-files review]]. Treat listed values as examples, not component specifications; the original course PDFs have been removed after review.

===== Planned outline =====

- What a resistor does: set current, create voltage drops, and dissipate energy as heat.
- Briefly explain standard preferred resistor values: E-series (such as E12, E24 and E96) provide progressively more nominal values within each decade. Show how to choose a nearby stocked value for a calculation, then check its tolerance and suitability; do not imply every series is available in every resistor type. Distinguish the E96 value series from the EIA-96 marking code used by some SMD resistors.
- Explain how to measure an individual resistor with a DMM: consult the meter manual for its resistance jack, mode, range and over-range indication; isolate the component from power, discharge capacitors safely, and remove it or lift one lead to avoid parallel paths. For a manual-range meter, select a range that includes the expected value; explain that range affects display resolution, while accuracy depends on the meter’s specifications too. Compare the reading with the marked nominal value and tolerance, for example, 0.988 kΩ is within a 1 kΩ ±5% resistor’s 950–1,050 Ω tolerance range. Do not measure a person’s body resistance or imply that one display symbol or jack arrangement applies to every meter.
- Explain four-band and five-band through-hole colour codes with worked examples for significant digits, multiplier and tolerance. Show how tolerance gives a possible range around the nominal resistance. Use band spacing as a reading-direction clue, but verify the scheme against a reliable chart; do not infer exact tolerance from band count alone. Note that some six-band schemes add temperature coefficient and SMD markings use separate, manufacturer-specific codes. Build on the existing booklet introduction rather than repeat it.
- Include the companion tool in ''tools/resistor-code-calculator.html'' when publishing the updated article. It decodes 4-, 5- and 6-band colour codes and common 3-digit, 4-digit, R-notation and EIA-96 SMD markings; explain that SMD codes vary and the exact part datasheet takes precedence.
- Compare resistor constructions—carbon composition, carbon film, metal film, thick- and thin-film SMD, and wirewound—by typical cost, nominal tolerance (initial accuracy), temperature coefficient, long-term stability, and use. Distinguish tolerance from drift with temperature and over time. Treat any trend as illustrative, not a universal ranking: exact performance and price depend on the part series, resistance, power rating, package, manufacturer, order quantity, and supplier. Base technical figures on exact manufacturer datasheets and check any UK price examples against current listings at publication.
- Add a brief datasheet note distinguishing maximum working voltage (continuous use) from any maximum overload or pulse voltage (short duration under specified conditions). Explain that these limits are separate from the power rating; do not treat an overload rating as permission for sustained overvoltage. Verify terminology and limits in the exact resistor datasheet.
- Explain lead-free resistor terminations and RoHS in UK electronics: distinguish a component’s lead-free claim from the solder alloy and from conformity of finished equipment. RoHS restricts specified substances in in-scope EEE; for lead, the maximum concentration is 0.1% by weight in homogeneous material, subject to scope and exemptions. Clarify that RoHS-compliant does not necessarily mean absolutely lead-free. Check the [[https://www.gov.uk/guidance/rohs-compliance-and-guidance|current GOV.UK RoHS guidance]] and GB/NI differences at publication.
- Choose a value and power rating for an LED indicator; explain the calculation and assumptions.
- Explain an unloaded potential divider using ''Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)'', with R1 above and R2 below the output node. Use the course’s 3 V, 1 kΩ/2 kΩ example to show 1 mA through the string and 2 V across R2. The transcript says R1 in one sentence, but the supplied diagram and its later ratio correctly show R2 in the numerator. Explain that a load connected across the output is in parallel with R2 and can change Vout; use the existing calculator to explore the ideal ratio, then distinguish it from a loaded circuit.
- Use a thermistor as one resistor in a potential divider to show how temperature changes can produce a changing voltage.
- Explain tolerance, temperature effects, and failure modes at a useful level.
- Explain that electrical power dissipated by a resistor becomes heat, and include a warning against deliberately overloading or touching a hot resistor.
- Explain that series resistances add and the same current flows through each part. Use three 1 kΩ ±5% resistors as an example: 3 kΩ nominal, with a worst-case range of 2.85–3.15 kΩ. For series or parallel combinations, calculate each resistor’s dissipation with ''P = I² × R'' and check each part’s power rating, derating, working voltage and temperature conditions; do not assign the same power to every resistor or add ratings without checking the actual parts and layout. Keep any Scout-facing worked circuit within the project’s 12 V DC supply limit. Mention that a parallel path can affect an in-circuit resistance measurement.
- Distinguish a mechanical potentiometer, a rheostat connection, and a digital potentiometer. Explain that a digital potentiometer is a low-level adjustable component with terminal-voltage, current and power limits, not a general high-voltage or high-power substitute for a resistor.
- Show the examples in Falstad, then compare the simulation with measurements from a real low-voltage circuit. If a visual comparison is useful, create an original sourced table or diagram rather than treating the supplied carbon-film/metal-film/wirewound graphic as a universal price and performance scale.

Explain Kirchhoff’s current and voltage laws in plain language or omit them here if they distract from practical resistor selection. * Coordinate with [[https://philipmcgaw.com/ohms-law-and-watts-law/|Is P = I x V Part of Ohm’s Law?]] and the fundamentals post: present ''V = I × R'' as the relationship for ohmic behaviour under stated conditions, and ''P = V × I'' as the electrical power relationship. Use both together for resistor heating and ratings, but explain the difference instead of calling power a rule of thumb.
* Link to the [[sites:scouts-electronics-badge:blog:electronics-fundamentals|electronics fundamentals post]] for the voltage, current, resistance, and power overview; keep this article focused on resistor choice and use.
* Use the Potential Divider Calculator as an interactive companion to the explanation. Check that its inputs, equations, units, and displayed results are clear, then show how changing either resistor affects the divider output.
* Philip is more likely to use thermistors than LDRs, so use a thermistor for the main sensor example. Check whether the selected part is NTC or PTC, and use its datasheet rather than assuming a universal resistance curve or divider value.
* Keep the sensor example inside the resistor article as an application of the potential divider. If Falstad does not model the selected thermistor, use a variable resistor only as a clearly labelled approximation of its changing resistance.
* The LED-resistor post has a table for several LED colours, three supply voltages, and three target currents. Carry the table forward only after rechecking each calculation against the assumed LED forward voltage and supply. Its blue and white LED examples at 3.3 V have very little voltage across the resistor, so explain why the current can vary significantly.
* Review of [[https://philipmcgaw.com/led-resistors-values/|LED Resistor Values]] (published 2024-03-13): keep its useful caveat that the listed LED forward voltages are estimates and datasheets should be checked. Recalculate every table entry and explain when a selected standard resistor is deliberately higher than the calculated value to keep current below a target. For example, the yellow LED row at 3.3 V and 5 mA uses an assumed 2.1 V forward voltage, which gives ''(3.3 V − 2.1 V) ÷ 0.005 A = 240 Ω''; the table lists 270 Ω, which gives about 4.4 mA. Say whether this is a conservative choice. Also qualify the statement that all listed values suit 1/4 W resistors: confirm resistor power and derating for the actual design and conditions. In the red LED row at a 12 V supply and 20 mA target, the listed 560 Ω resistor dissipates about 0.19 W with the stated 1.7 V LED drop, leaving little margin for a 1/4 W part.
* State the practical rule plainly: a bare LED needs current limiting. For the simple battery and microcontroller examples, use a correctly sized series resistor; never connect the LED directly across a supply or output pin. Note that some LED modules or driver circuits already provide current limiting, so check their documentation.
* Explain that increasing an LED’s forward current (''I_F'') usually increases brightness, but also increases heat and the risk of damage if the LED’s rating is exceeded. Record Philip’s practical rule of thumb: 20 mA is a sensible upper design value for many common 3 mm and 5 mm indicator LEDs, not a universal maximum; tell readers to check the specific LED’s datasheet and consider a lower current where it gives enough light.
* A useful worked example from the LED challenge is a 9 V supply, an assumed 2 V LED forward voltage, and a 15 mA target. This gives ''(9 V − 2 V) ÷ 0.015 A ≈ 467 Ω''; choose 470 Ω and estimate about 14.9 mA. Label the forward voltage and current as assumptions for that example, not universal LED ratings. Check the LED datasheet and supply tolerance, and check resistor dissipation too: about 0.10 W here, so select a power rating with suitable margin.
* In the solution explanation, apply Ohm’s law to the resistor using the voltage across that resistor, not the full supply voltage: subtract the LED’s forward voltage from the supply voltage. Explain that the same current flows through both series components. If troubleshooting a dark LED, advise disconnecting power before checking polarity or changing connections.
* The challenge sheet describes an LED as having “very little resistance” and asks readers to treat it as 0 Ω. Do not repeat that explanation: an LED has a non-linear current-voltage relationship, and its forward voltage depends on the device, current, and conditions. Use the forward-voltage model for this simple calculation, and check the actual LED datasheet. The transcript’s 20 mA maximum is specific to its stated example, not a universal LED limit.
* Recheck resistor power as well as LED current. For example, the published red LED, 12 V, 560 Ω case dissipates about 0.19 W in the resistor if the assumed LED drop is 1.7 V, so a 1/8 W resistor would be undersized; check the exact design and rating before recommending a part.
* Include a safety warning that a resistor can become hot when it dissipates power. The //Basic Electronics// source demonstrates this by overloading a low-value resistor from a battery and telling the reader to feel it; do not repeat that experiment. Explain heat using the power calculation and resistor rating, and do not ask readers to touch a component to judge its temperature.
* Explain how the actual LED datasheet, supply tolerance, target current, resistor tolerance, and resistor power rating affect the result. A simulation and a measured low-voltage example can show the difference between an estimate and a built circuit.
* Review the wording and equations carefully; comments on the existing post flag places where notation and terminology need clearer explanation.
* Keep specialised reliability models and failure-rate figures only if they serve the intended reader and can be checked against a suitable source.

* The image’s example resistance codes follow the three-digit pattern used in Bourns Trimpot product documentation: the first two digits are significant and the third is the power-of-ten multiplier. Present these as examples for a documented code system, not as a guarantee for every maker or component marking.
