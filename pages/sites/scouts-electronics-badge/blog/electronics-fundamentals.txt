====== Electronics fundamentals: voltage, current, resistance, and power ======

**Status:** Outline started; draft not written.

**Purpose:** Connect the basic electrical quantities to a simple circuit, then check the explanation with safe low-voltage measurements.

**Related article to review:** [[https://philipmcgaw.com/ohms-law-and-watts-law/|Is P = I x V Part of Ohm’s Law?]].

===== Planned outline =====

- Introduce electrical charge using a static-electricity example, then define current as charge passing a point per second and show that voltage drives current only around a complete circuit.
- Explain conductors and insulators with a wire's copper core and plastic covering, and show how conductivity varies by material and conditions.
- Define voltage as a potential difference between two points and explain why a stated voltage needs a reference point.
- Introduce a source, load, and conducting path; explain closed and open circuits, a switch, voltage across an open switch, and short-circuit risks.
- Define resistance as opposition to current, measured in ohms (''Ω''), and use water flow through pipes as a first analogy. Explain where the analogy stops being exact.
- Introduce Ohm’s law, ''V = I × R'', rearrange it to find current, and show the relationships ''V = I × R'' and ''P = V × I'' with a checked, low-voltage example.
- Introduce Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL): at a connected node, total current entering equals total current leaving. Use ''IA + IB = IC + ID'' for a clearly drawn junction, and explain that a wire crossing is not a connection unless marked according to the schematic convention.
- Introduce Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL): the directed sum of voltage rises and drops around a closed loop is zero. Choose a loop direction and keep voltage polarities consistent; use a simple battery-and-resistor loop to show how signed voltages balance.
- Introduce common SI prefixes used with electrical units, with examples of converting between milliamperes and amperes, and kilo-ohms and ohms.
- Explain that a DMM reading gives a numeric measurement at a time, while an oscilloscope displays voltage against time as a waveform. Use a simple, low-voltage signal to show changing behaviour and compare two points only when the scope channels and probe references can be connected safely. Treat the scope as a useful tool for time-varying signals, not a requirement for every circuit or a guarantee that a fault's cause is identified.
- Compare time- and frequency-domain views as two representations of a signal. Explain that periodic signals can be represented by Fourier series, while aperiodic signals can have a continuous Fourier spectrum; avoid saying every real signal is a finite collection of sinusoids. A pure sine has one frequency component in a simple one-sided magnitude plot; a DC offset appears at 0 Hz. Explain that a spectrum may plot magnitude or power, and that phase is another part of the transform even when the display shows magnitude only. Introduce the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) for a finite sampled record; the FFT is an efficient algorithm for calculating it, and the inverse DFT reconstructs the sampled time record. Keep derivations out of scope.
- Explain bandwidth in context: for a signal, define its occupied frequency range or stated measurement bandwidth; for a circuit, describe the range it passes or transmits. Use a square wave to show that periodic switching produces harmonics, and that an ideal 50% duty-cycle square wave has odd harmonics with decreasing amplitudes. A real circuit has finite edge rates and bandwidth, so observed shape depends on the source, interconnect and load. Avoid a universal bandwidth requirement; relate it to edge rate, timing, receiver thresholds and measurement setup. Keep this optional and advanced.
- Explain how transducers connect physical quantities to electrical signals, then introduce a signal generator as a controlled test source. If comparing it with a phone or computer audio output, explain that the latter is limited to audio frequencies and may attenuate square-wave harmonics; the displayed shape also depends on the output, adapter, load, cabling and measurement setup. Do not claim a generator is inherently clean or assume an audio output can safely drive an unknown circuit. Check exact equipment limits and keep any Scout demonstration low voltage.
- Introduce reactance and impedance using a sinusoidal example. Define ''X_C = 1/(2πfC)'' as the magnitude of ideal capacitive reactance and ''Z_C = 1/(j2πfC)'' as complex impedance; explain that impedance carries phase information and is not simply ''R + X'' as scalar magnitudes. Show that capacitor current leads its voltage by 90° for an ideal capacitor, and that a settled ideal capacitor blocks DC while a real capacitor has leakage. Note that real capacitors’ ESR and ESL change their behaviour near and above self-resonance. Distinguish resistance readings on a basic DMM from impedance measurements over frequency; keep the maths optional and advanced.
- Explain source/output impedance, load/input impedance and the voltage-divider effect when connecting stages. Distinguish voltage bridging (usually low source impedance driving a higher input impedance) from impedance matching for power transfer or transmission-line reflection control. Show that a 10:1 resistive load/source ratio gives about 91% of open-circuit voltage, while a 1000:1 ratio gives about 99.9%; frame 10:1 as a context-dependent rule of thumb, not a universal boundary, and don’t suggest 100:1 or 1000:1 is always required. For a simple resistive source, equal source/load resistance maximises delivered load power but is generally not the goal for voltage-signal interconnection; for reactive AC impedances, conjugate matching is the maximum-power condition. Keep RF transmission-line matching as an optional advanced note and explain that reflections are not a generic description of every lumped-circuit mismatch. Cite manufacturer/academic sources and specify the context for any matching example.
- Explain filter frequency response as voltage transfer (output amplitude divided by input amplitude) and phase shift versus frequency. Introduce a Bode plot with magnitude and phase graphs, usually against logarithmic frequency, and show how a sine-wave sweep can produce response points. Explain logarithmic axes: equal spacing represents equal ratios, with a decade as ×10 and an octave as ×2; a semi-log plot has one log axis and a log-log plot has two. Distinguish a log-scaled frequency axis from magnitude expressed in decibels, and note that zero frequency cannot be shown on a log axis. Keep voltage gain distinct from power gain, and explain that a passive filter does not provide net power gain while an active design can use its supply to provide gain. Avoid the blanket claim that every passive topology has voltage gain no greater than one; specify the circuit and measurement conditions. Treat the course’s low-pass values as reported examples because its pasted excerpt gives no schematic or component values. Check signal-generator output impedance, circuit loading, scope probes and channel phase matching before any practical comparison; keep Scout demonstrations within 12 V DC.
- Use an unloaded first-order RC low-pass as a worked frequency-response example: series R, capacitor to circuit common, and output across C. Derive ''f_c = 1/(2πRC)'' with its assumptions; calculate the nominal corner for 10 kΩ and 10 nF as about 1.59 kHz. At cutoff, the voltage ratio is ''1/√2'' (−3.01 dB relative to passband) and phase is −45°; above it, the asymptotic magnitude slope is −20 dB per decade. Make clear that cutoff is not a brick-wall boundary, and half-power interpretation assumes equal impedances. Note that source resistance, output loading and component tolerance change the actual response; the output phase lag is not a fixed time delay. Check exact source, scope and probe setup before demonstrating it, and keep Scout signals within 12 V DC.
- Add an unloaded first-order RC high-pass as the complementary worked example: series capacitor, resistor to circuit common, and output across the resistor. Use ''H(jω) = jωRC/(1 + jωRC)'' and ''f_c = 1/(2πRC)''; 10 kΩ and 10 nF again give about 1.59 kHz. At cutoff, the voltage ratio is ''1/√2'' (−3.01 dB relative to passband) and phase is +45°; below cutoff, the asymptotic rise is +20 dB per decade. Explain AC coupling and blocking a settled DC component, while noting a changing offset produces a transient and does not guarantee a zero-centred output in every circuit. Source resistance, output loading and component tolerance change the response. Half-power language assumes equal impedances. Use low-voltage generated signals and check source, scope and probe setup; Scout signals remain within 12 V DC.
- Use the “Filter audio” challenge and solution as a loaded low-pass design example, with a low-output-impedance audio source, series R, shunt C, and high-input-impedance amplifier. Include the loaded corner ''f_c = 1/[2πC((R_s + R) || R_L)]'' and passband divider loss. The course selects 10 kΩ/100 nF: about 159.2 Hz unloaded and 159.6 Hz for assumed 3 Ω source and 3 MΩ load, meeting the 140–160 Hz target; with a 1 MΩ input the pole is about 160.7 Hz and slightly misses it. State that real equipment impedances must be checked. The course reports about 0.71 voltage ratio at 150 Hz; the ideal unloaded ratio for its component values is about 0.728, so label the observed value approximate and setup-dependent. Explain that a one-pole RC filter rolls off at 20 dB per decade and is not a brick-wall filter or a complete speaker crossover; keep it at line/signal level, not between a power amplifier and speaker. Draw an original IEC-style schematic from the supplied frame: input left, series resistor, output node, shunt capacitor to circuit common below, and output measured across the capacitor. Use net labels and a layout note where physical placement matters; do not reuse the course screenshot. Use the Colorado filters and voltage-divider labs as technical references.
- Introduce decoupling as a practical use of capacitors on supply rails: a local capacitor can support changing IC current demand through a short supply-and-return loop. Explain that a capacitor does not simply “drain all noise to ground”; real impedance, layout, load and frequency matter. Treat 100 nF and larger values as examples, not a universal recipe, and direct readers to the exact IC/regulator datasheet and layout guide. Explain that capacitors grouped in a schematic still need deliberate physical placement on the board. Link to the [[sites:scouts-electronics-badge:blog:decoupling-capacitors|dedicated decoupling article]] for advanced detail; no arbitrary capacitor-sprinkling activity for Scouts.
- Introduce decibels as logarithmic ratios: ''10 log10(P₂/P₁)'' for power ratios and ''20 log10(V₂/V₁)'' for voltage-level or transfer ratios. Explain that voltage dB equals the power-ratio result only for equal impedances; otherwise account for impedance when converting voltage to power. Explain that dB is relative, while dBm, dBW and dBV specify references (1 mW, 1 W and 1 V respectively); use correct case and state RMS for AC voltage references. Include 0 dB = unity, approximately +3 dB = twice the power, and +6 dB = twice the voltage amplitude (about four times the power at equal impedances). Explain that cascaded gains in dB add when definitions and loading are consistent, while absolute dBm levels are not gains. Mention that zero ratio tends to −∞ dB but practical instruments have a noise floor. Keep this optional and advanced, with RF examples clearly distinguished from Scout activities.
- If demonstrating an FFT, note briefly that sample rate and record length determine the displayed span and frequency-bin spacing, and that finite records can spread energy between bins (spectral leakage); windowing changes that display. Treat the scope FFT as a view of sampled data, not a perfect spectrum analyzer.
- Add an optional RF/EMC note that a spectrum analyser displays signal level against frequency and complements an oscilloscope’s time-domain view. Scope FFTs and spectrum analysers both show spectra, but their architectures, input ranges, capture bandwidth, dynamic range and measurement settings differ by model. Introduce span, resolution bandwidth (RBW) and detector only as controls that affect what the display reveals; never imply a scope FFT or an analyser trace alone establishes EMC compliance. Retain this as advanced material, reflecting Philip’s regular RF/EMC spectrum-analyser work.
- Compare DC and AC as circuit behaviours: DC current has one direction, while AC current reverses direction and voltage polarity periodically. Explain that DC need not be perfectly constant and AC need not be a sine wave. Use conventional current direction in circuit diagrams; if mentioning electron drift, label it as opposite in metal wires. Explain that mains adapters convert UK AC supply to the DC used by many electronic circuits, without implying that every part of a device uses DC. Give 230 V nominal and 50 Hz as UK context only, with authoritative references; state clearly that Scouts must not build or measure mains circuits and practical Scout supplies remain limited to 12 V DC.
- Add an optional UK installation footnote distinguishing supply voltage from voltage drop in the installation wiring. The ESQCR declares 230 V phase-to-neutral at low-voltage supply terminals; BS 7671 separately gives wiring voltage-drop guidance. IET guidance describes recommended values of 3% for lighting and 5% for other uses on a public low-voltage supply (6.9 V and 11.5 V at 230 V). Explain that this is a design allowance between the installation origin and load, not the DNO supply tolerance, and that equipment requirements may be stricter. Before publication, verify Regulation 27 and its permitted variation in the current ESQCR, and confirm the voltage-drop wording and values in current BS 7671:2018+A4:2026. Keep the note descriptive, not a Scout activity.
- Explain sine-wave peak and peak-to-peak amplitude, frequency in hertz, period in seconds with ''T = 1/f'', and phase difference between same-frequency waves. Show that a quarter-period shift is 90°. For a centred sine wave, ''Vpp = 2Vp''; distinguish its peak amplitude from any DC offset and use “peak amplitude”, not “semi-amplitude”. Use a checked low-voltage example and avoid implying that sound, ocean or light waves are always sinusoidal.
- Explain RMS voltage as ''V_RMS = sqrt(mean(v(t)^2))'', the equivalent DC voltage that gives the same average heating in a resistor. For a zero-centred sine wave only, ''V_RMS = Vp/sqrt(2)''; for example, a 2 V peak sine wave is about 1.41 V RMS. The general RMS definition also applies to other waveforms, but the sine-wave shortcut does not. Explain that an AC DMM may be average-responding or true-RMS; readings on non-sinusoidal signals depend on meter type and its specified bandwidth, crest factor and measurement mode. Use a low-voltage signal generator or simulation for any demonstration. Do not probe a wall outlet or turn mains measurement into a Scout activity.
- Add an optional RF note on wavelength: define ''λ'' as the distance between corresponding points in successive cycles and use ''λ = v_p/f''. In free space, ''v_p = c''; on a cable or PCB transmission line, use the line’s phase velocity (often expressed relative to free-space speed as a velocity factor), so do not assume free-space wavelength. Keep this beyond the Scouts’ core material.
- Compare an ideal voltage source with a real battery: explain that a real source has limits and its terminal voltage can change with load.
- Introduce the digital multimeter’s display, selector, probes, sockets, and common functions; explain how to select a meter based on the measurements and safety needs.
- Measure voltage across a component and current through the circuit with a multimeter, following the booklet's meter-safety steps. Explain accuracy, precision, repeatability and display resolution simply; more digits or repeatable readings do not by themselves prove that a result is accurate.
- If demonstrating an oscilloscope, check the exact scope and probe input limits and grounding arrangement first. Keep Scout work to checked low-voltage circuits; do not connect a scope probe ground to an arbitrary circuit point or use this activity for mains measurements.
- Build the example on a solderless breadboard; explain how contact groups and the centre gap work, and check whether the power rows are split before connecting the supply.
- Use Falstad to explore the circuit and compare the model with measurements from the real low-voltage build.
- Link to the resistor article for power ratings and the battery article for capacity and voltage under load.
- Add an optional advanced note explaining circuit common, Earth reference, and floating sources without bringing mains wiring into scope.
- Add an optional advanced note that a voltage reference provides a known comparison level for analogue circuits such as ADCs and comparators. Keep the distinction from circuit common and power supply rails clear; if discussing reference quality, introduce initial accuracy, temperature change, input/load effects and noise at a high level.
- Conclude by encouraging readers to keep applying the fundamentals as their circuits grow more complex and use them to troubleshoot; point to practical next steps.

* For AC/DC, explain direction and periodic reversal without implying that all DC is constant or all AC is sinusoidal. Describe 230 V nominal, 50 Hz UK mains only as background, cited to NESO and UK Power Networks; do not make it an activity or suggest measuring it. Keep Scout circuit supplies at or below 12 V DC.

===== Notes to carry forward =====

* The Scouts booklet already gives short definitions and the formulae. The blog post should add a worked example, a practical measurement, and a clear explanation of the limits of the water analogy rather than repeat the definitions at length.
* A solderless breadboard makes a useful bridge between a schematic and a working low-voltage circuit. Explain the common five-hole contact groups and centre gap as typical layouts, not a guarantee for every board; check each board's markings or continuity because power rows may be split. Power down before changing connections. This practical setup and fault-finding angle is drawn from the breadboard chapter in the reviewed //Electronics For Dummies// source; do not reuse its figures.
* Explain static charge as a transfer of electrons between materials, not charge created by friction. Use conventional current direction (+ to −) throughout; put the opposite electron drift in metal wires in a clearly labelled advanced note.
* Explain that conductors and insulators are useful categories on a range of conductivity, and that temperature can affect conductivity differently in different materials. Use the copper-core and plastic-insulation example; do not use optical transparency as a stand-in for electrical conductivity.
* Treat voltage as a difference between two points, not a property of one point on its own. Explain that ''0 V'', common, and ground name a chosen reference in a circuit; this does not necessarily mean a connection to the Earth. Avoid the transcript's North American mains example. Use a checked, low-voltage battery example.
* For an optional advanced note, distinguish a circuit's chosen common/reference from protective Earth. A battery-powered circuit is normally floating unless it is connected to another reference; two separate floating circuits do not automatically share a reference. In the UK and EU, most AC-to-DC wall adapters have isolated, floating low-voltage outputs, so their output common is not normally Earth-referenced. Check the exact product documentation rather than assuming how a particular adapter is built. Never advise joining adapter outputs to make a higher voltage; only use supplies in combinations their manufacturers explicitly allow. Keep mains-side wiring out of scope. The pasted transcript's claim that a 5 V adapter's neutral is Earth-referenced is not a safe generalisation, and physically connecting floating sources is not inherently safe without checking the circuit design.
* Explain that current is not used up by a lamp: in a simple series loop, the same conventional current flows through each part while electrical energy is transferred. Do not claim all current is a flow of holes; hole current is a model used for semiconductors.
* Distinguish measurement accuracy (closeness to a reference value), precision (agreement among repeated measurements under stated conditions), repeatability, and display resolution. A meter can give consistent readings that are offset from the reference, and extra displayed digits do not guarantee better accuracy. Keep formal uncertainty analysis beyond the beginner explanation.
* Do not describe electrons as choosing an “easier” route. A short circuit is an unintended low-resistance path that can draw excessive current; explain that current division depends on the complete circuit, not on electrons making choices.
* Assume the reader is new to electronics. Build understanding before introducing equations, keep the mathematics to basic arithmetic, and use only the calculations needed to show how the quantities relate.
* Teach SI prefixes as powers of ten and emphasise that prefix symbols are case-sensitive: ''M'' means mega (one million), while ''m'' means milli (one thousandth). Cover kilo, mega, giga, milli, micro, nano, and pico, then use practical conversions such as ''42 mA = 0.042 A'' and ''24 kΩ = 24,000 Ω''. Choose the prefix that makes a value easy to read, and retain a space between the number and unit (''24 kΩ'').
* The 2021 course SI-prefix handout, summarised in the [[sites:scouts-electronics-badge:sources:electronics-foundations-fundamentals-2021-review|course materials review]], covers the same common prefixes. Its table shows uppercase ''K'' for kilo; retain the correct lowercase ''k'' in the booklet and check prefix symbols against the [[https://www.bipm.org/en/measurement-units/si-prefixes|BIPM SI prefix table]]. The original course PDFs have been removed from the repository after review.
* For the resistance example, ''9 V ÷ 20 Ω = 0.45 A'' is arithmetically correct for an ideal, fixed 20 Ω resistance. Label it as a calculation example, not a recommended bulb circuit: a real lamp’s resistance changes as its filament heats, and the exercise parts list includes a 6.15 V, 0.5 A bulb, so do not imply it should be connected directly to 9 V. Explain that Ohm’s law relates voltage, current, and resistance; a fixed resistance gives a proportional relationship only while its resistance remains effectively constant.
* Coordinate with the existing [[https://philipmcgaw.com/ohms-law-and-watts-law/|Ohm’s law and Watt’s law article]]: distinguish ''V = I × R'' from the electrical power relationship ''P = V × I'', while explaining why they are often used together. Do not say resistance is always constant; the simple proportional form applies to ohmic behaviour under specified conditions, and components such as filament lamps change resistance as they heat. Avoid making the historical naming distinction more prominent than the practical calculation.
* Explain energy as something transferred and transformed in a circuit: a battery transfers energy from its chemical store, and loads may change electrical energy into light, heat, sound, or movement. Define energy in joules (J) and power as the rate of energy transfer, with ''1 W = 1 J/s''. In ''P = V × I'', use the voltage across and current through the component. The simple LED example can show component power: at a 15 mA target, 7 V across the resistor gives 105 mW, and 2 V across the LED gives 30 mW; together they equal the 135 mW supplied at 9 V. Label these as idealised example values. Avoid the transcript’s claim that current is kinetic energy or its use of voltage as a static build-up of charge; voltage is a potential difference. Do not repeat the Einstein attribution for energy conservation without a reliable source.
* Keep the hose analogy as a rough model: pressure difference can stand in for voltage, flow rate for current, and restrictions for resistance. Do not say a bend in a hose maps directly to a particular electrical resistance or imply that all components have fixed resistance. Return to the complete circuit when explaining how changing voltage or resistance affects current.
* Keep examples to checked, low-voltage circuits. The adult blog may discuss a sealed, pre-built mains adapter only from its low-voltage output side; do not describe mains measurements, opening or modifying the adapter, or constructing a power supply. Keep adapters out of the Scouts battery-only activity.
* The pasted safety segment gives an approximate 50 V threshold and calls its example circuits safe to follow. The user clarifies that the 50 V figure belongs to the SELV context in BS 7671; SELV includes separation requirements, so the voltage value alone is not a general safety assurance. Do not treat it as the Scout project limit. The project rule is a maximum 12 V DC supply output for Scout circuit activities, including a battery pack's maximum charged voltage. HSE says contact above about 50 V AC can cause injury, but risk varies with circumstances; low voltage can still create hazards such as sparks or battery short-circuit heating. Scouts requires risk assessment for every activity. Link to current UK guidance and assess the actual task; do not describe a circuit as safe simply because it uses a low voltage. See [[https://www.hse.gov.uk/electricity/faq.htm|HSE electrical safety FAQs]], [[https://www.hse.gov.uk/electricity/injuries.htm|HSE electrical injuries]], [[https://electrical.theiet.org/bs-7671-18th-edition-wiring-regulations|IET information on the current edition of BS 7671]], and the [[https://www.scouts.org.uk/por/2a-key-policies/2a8-safety-policy/|Scouts Safety Policy]].
* Check the available current and risks of short circuits, heating, stored energy, and the work environment. Keep all mains work outside the scope of this post and the Scout activity.
* Explain that an ideal voltage source is a model that holds its voltage constant as the load changes. A real battery has internal resistance and current limits; the load determines the current drawn, and the battery's maximum current is not a current it automatically supplies. If a load demands more than the battery can safely provide, its terminal voltage may fall and it may heat or be damaged. Check any quantitative example against the selected battery and load data, and never test the limit by shorting a battery or attaching an unsuitable load.
* Correct the transcript's arithmetic before using its example: ''9 V ÷ 10 kΩ = 0.9 mA'', not 9 mA. Its ideal calculation for a 2 Ω load is 4.5 A, but do not imply a generic 9 V battery can supply that current or use it as a practical test. The stated 1 A capability is hypothetical unless supported by the exact source data. Exclude the wall-adapter example from the Scouts booklet; the adult blog may discuss a sealed adapter only at its low-voltage output (see the sizing note below).
* Include Philip’s adapter-sizing practice as a personal rule of thumb for adult projects: choose a suitable low-voltage DC adapter whose rated output power is at least 25% above the circuit’s maximum expected power demand. Check that its output voltage, polarity, connector, and regulation suit the circuit; a higher current rating is available capacity, not current forced into the load. State that this 25% margin is Philip’s practice, not a universal requirement. If overloaded, an adapter’s output may sag, current-limit, shut down, open a protective fuse, or be damaged; the behaviour depends on its design, so do not claim that every adapter fails in the same way.
* Explain multimeter connections precisely: voltage is measured in parallel; current is measured in series. Follow the meter's instructions and the existing booklet safety guidance.
* Add a practical DC-voltage walkthrough: choose the DC volts setting (''VDC'' or ''V⎓''), use the voltage socket, and on a manual-range meter select a range above the expected value (start higher and step down if unsure, following the meter manual). A 20 V range suits a 9 V battery when available. Measure across the source or component. Explain that the display is the red-probe potential relative to the black probe; reversing the probes normally changes the sign, not the magnitude. Call black the reference probe for that reading rather than implying it must always be connected to ground. Keep probe tips from shorting adjacent contacts, and leave AC-voltage operation outside the Scout activity.
* Add a carefully supervised DC-current measurement using an approved battery circuit: switch off before opening the circuit, put the leads in the sockets specified for the expected current, confirm the input/range/fuse rating against the meter manual, select DC current, and insert the meter in series. Power the circuit only after the Leader checks the setup; switch off before removing the meter and return the red lead to the voltage socket. Explain that a reversed current-probe orientation normally gives a negative reading. Do not copy the transcript’s phrase that the resistor is the “source of current”; describe conventional current around the whole circuit. Explain that a current input has a small burden voltage rather than treating the meter as an ideal wire. Never connect a meter in current mode across a supply; it can short the supply, blow the fuse, or damage the meter. A fuse is protection, not a substitute for correct setup.
* The course DMM handout (summarised in the [[sites:scouts-electronics-badge:sources:electronics-foundations-fundamentals-2021-review|2021 course-files review]]) listed SparkFun TOL-12966 and Extech MN36 with US-oriented product links, but gave no specification table. The handout PDF has been removed at the user's request. The pasted transcript names functions and ports for these examples; check the current meter manuals before repeating model-specific details, and do not treat the old links as UK buying recommendations. Explain that socket layouts vary. For current measurements, emphasise series connection, the correct current input, range, and fuse status as stated in the meter manual; require Leader supervision, switching off before moving leads, and returning the red lead to the voltage/resistance socket.
* If the simulation does not model the real source's limits, describe the simplification and do not present the simulation as a measurement of the physical battery.
* Include an optional oscilloscope signal-reading exercise using a verified low-voltage source and the exact scope/probe manuals. Match the probe’s 1X/10X switch to the channel ratio, check compensation, select DC coupling for the whole signal, and use the calibration output to introduce volts/div and time/div. Work out peak-to-peak voltage and period from divisions; explain that the physical ground clip may be Earth-connected and must go only to an appropriate circuit common. Keep the Scout supply at or below 12 V and exclude mains measurement. Explain that probe loading and limits vary by model.
* Explain that the oscilloscope grid’s vertical divisions represent voltage, with the selected scale (V/div) setting the voltage per division; horizontal divisions represent time, with the time base (s/div) setting time per division. Vertical scale and position can be set per channel; horizontal scale and position affect all channels together on the instructor’s Rigol DS1054/DS1054Z. Check the exact manual because controls vary by scope. Treat an unconnected input’s fluctuations as pickup/noise from the measurement setup, not proof of circuit noise. Avoid arbitrary tiny-scale settings; select scales and probe settings within the signal, scope, and probe limits.
* Explain edge triggering as aligning or starting a captured record when the chosen signal crosses a set level on a rising or falling edge. Show how trigger source, slope and level affect a stable display. Compare normal mode (waits for a trigger), auto mode (can update when no trigger arrives, so the display may not be synchronised), and single mode (captures once and stops), following the exact scope manual. Distinguish trigger Auto mode from the Auto/Autoset display button. Use a known, low-voltage signal; a wandering display or visible jitter may come from signal variation, noise or trigger setup. A single capture of a burst can be inspected after acquisition; do not infer that tapping a probe onto a terminal is a reliable signal source.
