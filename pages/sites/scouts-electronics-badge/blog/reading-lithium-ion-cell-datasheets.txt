====== Reading a lithium-ion cell datasheet ======

A chemistry label is a starting clue, not a complete specification. If you are assessing a lithium-ion cell for an existing product or pack, the exact manufacturer datasheet tells you what the cell is designed to do and the limits that apply. A number printed in a comparison chart cannot replace it.

===== Start with the exact model =====

Find the full model number and manufacturer, then check it against the manufacturer’s own documentation. Similar-looking cells can have different capacity, dimensions, terminals, discharge limits, and charge conditions. If the model number cannot be matched to a credible datasheet, do not guess from the wrapper.

As one example, Panasonic’s industrial catalogue lists the NCR18650GA as an 18650-size cell with a nominal voltage of 3.6 V and a typical capacity of 3,450 mAh. It also states a 4.20 V charge condition for the listed lithium-ion cells. Those figures describe that product listing; they do not establish the voltage, capacity, or current capability of every cell labelled ''NCR'', or even every 18650 cell.

===== Read capacity with its test conditions =====

Capacity is not a single context-free number. Look for the discharge current, temperature, and end-of-discharge voltage used for the capacity test. A different load or cut-off voltage can produce a different result. The product’s usable capacity may also be lower than the datasheet figure if its equipment stops operating before the cell reaches the test cut-off.

A high capacity figure does not tell you the cell’s safe maximum current. Find the manufacturer’s continuous discharge limit and any pulse limit, including how long a pulse can last and the required time between pulses. Check whether those limits are reduced at high or low temperatures. Do not treat a pulse rating as a continuous rating.

===== Check charge and discharge limits =====

The datasheet should specify the charge method and voltage limit, discharge cut-off, allowed current, and temperature range. Follow the exact cell instructions. A charger that suits one lithium-ion cell or chemistry may be unsuitable for another. A protection circuit or battery management system is part of the product design; the presence of a circuit board does not make an unknown cell safe to use.

For a finished battery pack, follow the pack manufacturer’s limits and use its approved charger. The pack can have lower current or temperature limits than the cells inside it because of its wiring, enclosure, sensors, or protection system. Do not open or modify a pack to identify its cells.

===== Why the UK context matters =====

Use products intended for the UK market and follow their manufacturer instructions. UK product-safety guidance for e-bike batteries sets out specific expectations for that product category; it is not a general design recipe for every lithium-ion battery. The National Fire Chiefs Council gives consumer guidance on buying, charging, and disposing of lithium-ion products.

===== A practical checklist =====

Before relying on a cell specification, confirm: the manufacturer and full model number; chemistry and nominal voltage; charge voltage and method; capacity test current, temperature, and cut-off; continuous and pulse discharge limits; temperature range; and whether the part is a bare cell or a protected, finished pack.

If any of those details are missing, stop and seek the manufacturer’s documentation or use a complete product designed for the intended device. Do not assemble a pack from loose lithium-ion cells using a generic online chart.
