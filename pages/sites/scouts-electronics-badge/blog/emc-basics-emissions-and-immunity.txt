====== EMC basics: emissions, immunity and interference ======

**Status:** Outline started; UK market guidance needs a publication-time check.

**Audience:** Adult makers and older learners, with a plain-language summary suitable for Leaders.

===== Purpose =====

Explain why electronic products should coexist with nearby equipment: they should not cause unacceptable electromagnetic disturbance, and they should keep working in the disturbances expected in their intended environment. Separate the core terms from the tests and legal requirements used to assess a finished product.

===== Planned outline =====

- Introduce an everyday example: a noisy device disrupting a radio, or a radio-frequency field upsetting a poorly protected circuit.
- Define **EMC** as the ability of equipment to function as intended in its electromagnetic environment without causing intolerable disturbance to other equipment.
- Explain **emissions** as disturbance a device produces, and **immunity** as how well it operates when disturbance is present. Explain that **EMI** usually names the interference/disturbance problem; some industry material uses **EMS** for susceptibility or immunity, but the terms are not always used consistently.
- Distinguish conducted and radiated disturbance, and briefly introduce coupling paths: shared wires/grounds, electric or magnetic fields, and antennas/cables.
- Explain that EMC assessment covers both emissions and immunity, and depends on the product, intended environment, operating modes, configuration and applicable standards.
- Give the UK context carefully: Great Britain’s EMC Regulations 2016 set essential requirements for equipment placed on the market; Northern Ireland has separate guidance. A project prototype or one successful bench check does not establish product conformity.
- Explain the difference between design debugging, a pre-compliance check, and a formal conformity assessment or accredited test. Pre-compliance measurements help identify problems but do not prove compliance.
- Close with practical habits: use the intended cables and enclosure, test realistic modes, keep records, and seek a competent EMC lab or engineer when product-market obligations apply.

===== Sources and assessment =====

* UK Government, [[https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/electromagnetic-compatibility-regulations-2016/electromagnetic-compatibility-regulations-2016-great-britain|Electromagnetic Compatibility Regulations 2016: Great Britain]]. Primary guidance for GB product requirements; check separate NI guidance if relevant and recheck before publication.
* UK legislation, [[https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2016/1091/contents|Electromagnetic Compatibility Regulations 2016]]. The statutory definition and essential requirements distinguish disturbance generated from immunity to expected disturbance.
* European Commission, [[https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/electrical-and-electronic-engineering-industries-eei/electromagnetic-compatibility-emc-directive_en|EMC Directive overview]]. Useful for EU context; do not conflate EU and GB market routes.
* Nemko, [[https://www.nemko.com/services/product-testing/emc-testing|EMC testing service overview]]. A commercial laboratory page that clearly illustrates emissions and immunity as two parts of testing; do not use its marketing copy as a standards source.
* PCB HERO, [[https://www.pcb-hero.com/blogs/lilycolumn/what-s-the-difference-between-emi-ems-and-emc|“What’s the Difference between EMI EMS and EMC?”]]. Topic prompt only. Its definitions are inconsistent and should not be reused as technical explanations.
