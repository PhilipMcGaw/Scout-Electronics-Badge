====== Decoupling capacitors: do you need three values? ======

**Status:** First draft; technical review needed before publication.

**Source prompt:** Eric Bogatin, Larry Smith and Steve Sandler, [[https://www.signalintegrityjournal.com/articles/1589-the-myth-of-three-capacitor-values|“The Myth of Three Capacitor Values”]], //Signal Integrity Journal//, 2020-03-03.

===== The familiar recipe =====

Many circuit diagrams show several capacitors between a supply rail and ground. A familiar recipe is to place three values at each IC supply pin, often with each value ten times the next: for example, 10 µF, 1 µF and 100 nF. Another common instruction is to put a 100 nF capacitor beside every IC.

These can be useful starting points, and a particular chip may require them. But neither recipe is a law of electronics. The right components depend on the IC, the power supply, the circuit board and the changes in current the circuit must handle.

===== Why capacitors are put near ICs =====

When an IC switches, its supply current can change quickly. The wires and copper tracks between the power supply and the IC have resistance and inductance, so they cannot deliver every fast current change without some voltage disturbance. A nearby capacitor can supply some of that short-term current through a small local loop.

The connection matters as much as the printed capacitance value. Long tracks and extra vias add inductance to the loop. Place a recommended capacitor close to the supply and ground pins, and follow the manufacturer’s layout guidance. A capacitor several centimetres away may not do the same job as one beside the IC.

===== The schematic is not the PCB layout =====

A schematic shows which points are electrically connected; the positions of its symbols do not show where parts must sit on the board. To keep a drawing readable, decoupling capacitors may be grouped together or drawn away from the IC they serve. On the PCB, put each capacitor where the IC documentation requires it, close to the relevant supply and return pins. If that placement is easy to miss from the schematic, add a note such as “Place C1 close to IC1 supply pins” or call it out in a separate layout drawing. This matches the project’s schematic convention: record important physical layout information explicitly instead of relying on symbol positions or wire lengths.

===== A real capacitor is not ideal =====

A simple model of a capacitor includes capacitance, resistance and inductance. At lower frequencies, capacitance usually dominates: increasing capacitance lowers the capacitor’s impedance. At higher frequencies, the inductance of the part and its board connections becomes more important. Above its self-resonant frequency, the part behaves mainly like an inductor.

This explains the origin of the old “small capacitor for high frequencies, large capacitor for low frequencies” shortcut. With some older leaded capacitor constructions, smaller values came in physically smaller parts with shorter leads, and could have lower inductance. But capacitance alone does not tell you a modern surface-mount ceramic capacitor’s high-frequency performance. Package, construction, mounting and the current path all matter.

There is another practical detail: some high-capacitance ceramic capacitors lose effective capacitance when DC voltage is applied. The amount depends on the exact part, its dielectric, case size, rated voltage and operating voltage. Check the manufacturer’s data for the part under the conditions where it will be used.

===== Why three different values are not automatically better =====

Putting capacitors in parallel changes the impedance of the supply network. Because each part and its connections have their own resistance and inductance, combinations can create resonances: frequency ranges where impedance rises instead of falling. So adding more values does not guarantee a smoother or lower-impedance supply at every frequency.

The Signal Integrity Journal article challenges the automatic use of three decade-spaced values, especially where the reasoning assumes that a smaller MLCC must be the “high-frequency” part. Its central engineering lesson is sound: the capacitor network has to be considered with the regulator, IC package, board layout and load. The article’s particular comparisons and recommendations depend on its assumed models and system; they should not be treated as a universal replacement recipe.

===== What to do in a real design =====

Start with the datasheet and layout guide for the exact IC. They may specify capacitance, capacitor type, voltage rating, placement and number of parts. Keep those requirements unless the manufacturer or a properly checked redesign supports a change.

Then check the power supply and the rest of the circuit. A regulator may require a particular range of output capacitance or equivalent series resistance for stability. A processor or radio may draw fast bursts of current. A board connector may need bulk capacitance nearby. These are different jobs, and one capacitor value need not solve all of them.

For a demanding design, engineers can model or measure the power distribution network and check the supply voltage at the IC while it operates. A simple multimeter reading will not show every fast disturbance. A design that powers up once is not necessarily robust across different boards, temperatures, component tolerances and operating conditions.

For a beginner’s board, the practical rule is simpler: use the exact component values and placement shown in the board or IC documentation. If you are making your own circuit, learn from the reference design, but do not copy a “three capacitors” pattern without understanding what the chosen IC needs.

===== Takeaway =====

Decoupling is about giving a circuit a suitable low-impedance supply path over the frequencies and current changes that matter. Capacitor values are part of that design, but placement, package, real component behaviour and the rest of the power network matter too. Three different values may be right for one design; they are not a universal rule.

===== Source notes =====

* Bogatin, Smith and Sandler’s 2020 article is a specialist discussion of power distribution networks and MLCC decoupling. Use it to question inherited rules, not as a general instruction to remove or replace capacitors.
* Texas Instruments’ device hardware and layout guides illustrate why recommendations are device-specific: some require specified capacitor values and close placement at particular power pins. Always use documentation for the exact device and revision.
