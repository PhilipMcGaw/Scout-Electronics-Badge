# Why does a rubbed balloon stick to a wall?

**Status:** Draft started.

**Purpose:** Explain electrical charge, electron transfer, attraction, and static electricity through a simple balloon demonstration. The explanation should be understandable to readers aged 10–16 while giving enough detail to connect the demonstration to basic electronics.

## Draft

Rub a balloon on a woollen jumper, then hold it near a wall. The balloon may stick for a while. It looks like a magic trick, but the effect comes from electrical charge.

### The balloon and jumper exchange electrons

Everything is made from atoms. Atoms contain positively charged protons and negatively charged electrons. Most objects have balanced positive and negative charge overall, so we describe them as neutral.

When the balloon is rubbed against the jumper, the two materials come into close contact. Some electrons can transfer between them. With a rubber balloon and wool, electrons commonly move from the wool to the balloon. The balloon then has extra electrons and a net negative charge. The jumper has lost some electrons, so it has a net positive charge. The exact direction and amount of transfer depend on the materials and conditions.

The rubbing does not create charge. It moves charge from one material to another. The total charge is still balanced: the balloon's extra negative charge corresponds to the jumper's positive charge.

### Opposite charges attract

Like charges repel each other, while opposite charges attract. The negatively charged balloon and positively charged jumper therefore pull towards each other.

The wall can be neutral overall and still attract the balloon. The balloon's negative charge shifts the charge in atoms near the wall's surface slightly: the electrons move a little farther from the balloon, leaving the nearby surface slightly more positive than the area behind it. The wall's total charge has not changed, but this small shift is enough for the balloon and wall to attract. This effect is called polarisation.

The same idea explains why a charged balloon can attract small pieces of paper. The paper is neutral overall, but its charges shift slightly while the balloon is nearby.

### Why does the balloon stop sticking?

The charge does not stay in place for ever. It gradually spreads or leaks away through the air or to objects the balloon touches. Moist air usually lets charge leak away faster than dry air, so the effect can be weaker on a damp day. Once the charge imbalance is too small, the balloon no longer sticks.

### Static electricity and current

Static electricity describes a build-up or imbalance of charge. An electric current is charge moving through a path, such as a metal wire in a circuit. A tiny spark is a sudden movement of charge through the air as the imbalance is reduced.

Static sparks can damage electronic components. Keep this demonstration away from exposed circuit boards and sensitive electronics, and do not make sparks near flammable vapours.

## Demonstration notes

- Use a balloon and a woollen jumper or another suitable wool fabric. Rub them together, then test whether the balloon attracts the jumper, a wall, or small pieces of paper.
- Repeat the test without rubbing the balloon first. Compare what happens.
- The result can vary with the balloon, fabric, humidity, and surface. Describe this as an observation, not a guaranteed outcome.
- Do not use a balloon if anyone taking part has a latex allergy; choose a suitable non-latex balloon or discuss the demonstration without handling one.
